## 2. Molecular orbitals
- Quantum theory can also tell us about bonding in molecules
	- This lecture focuses on **homodiatomic** molecules
	- They are molecules made from **two identical** atoms.
### 2.1. Bond formation in hydrogen
- Each hydrogen atom has one electron that occupies the lowest energy **atomic** orbital (AO): the 1s orbital.
- When these two atoms come together they 'combine' and form **molecular** orbitals (MOs) between them.
	- The molecular orbitals also have energy levels.
	- They are labelled accordingly, in this case, σ (sigma) and σ* ('sigma star').
		- * refers to **antibonding**.
- These molecular orbitals **spread across the entire molecule**.
- They are still filled up according to **Pauli and Aufbau**.
	- In this case, the two electrons fill into the **σ orbital and will have opposite spins**, just like atomic orbitals.
- The bonding orbital has a **lower energ**y than the atomic orbitals.
	- So forming bonds **stabilise the electrons.**
### 2.2. Appearance of atomic orbitals
- **Similar to atomic orbitals**, the molecular orbitals are also **allowed wavefunctions**, but for the molecule.
- It is also **too difficult** to solve the Schrodinger for molecules, so approximations, or computational calculations are used instead.
#### 2.2.1. MOs as a linear combination of atomic orbitals
- MOs can be approximated by considering them as **linear combinations of atomic orbitals** (LCAOs).
- Atomic orbitals are the allowed wavefunctions for one electron.
	- Therefore they have **wave characteristics**.
- Therefore they can add **constructively** or **destructively** like waves.
	- Two i**n-phase** waves add constructively (**constructive interference**) to give a wave with **larger amplitude**.
		- In-phase means the **phases of the two atomic orbitals match.**
	- Two **out-of-phase** waves add destructively (**destructive interference**) add to give a wave with **smaller (or zero) amplitude.**
-  ***N* AOs will combine to form *N* MOs.**
#### 2.2.2. Appearance of MOs for hydrogen
- In hydrogen molecules (H<sub>2</sub>), the two 1s AOs can combine **constructively** or **destructively** to give **two MOs.**
- When the two 1s **constructively** add (**in-phase**):
	- The two 1s merge to form a 'sausage-shaped' MO.
	- It will be labelled as 1σ<sub>g</sub>.
		- $\psi _{1\sigma_{g}} = \psi_{1s}(H_A)+\psi_{1s}(H_B)$
- When the two 1s **destructively** add (**out-of-phase**):
	- The two 1s merge to form a MO with **two outward-pointing 'lobes'.**
	- As the half-way point, the two AOs cancel out exactly and there would be a **nodal plane**. (See [[CH164/Part 2 - Quantum mechanics/L8]])
	- The two lobes each have a **phase**. (+ve and -ve).
	- It will be labelled as 1σ<sub>u</sub>* 
		- $\psi _{1\sigma_{u}^*} = \psi_{1s}(H_A)-\psi_{1s}(H_B)$
- The wavefunction of the MO is the sum of the wavefunctions of the AO.
#### 2.2.3. Labelling MOs
- MO labels follow a specific structure
- Take 1σ<sub>u</sub>* 
	- 1 means that this is the lowest energy σ<sub>u</sub> MO
	- σ means that the MO is spherically symmetrical when viewed down the inter-nuclear axis.
	- u means that there is a sign change when inversed
	- * means that the orbital is antibonding.
## 3. Molecular orbital energy level diagrams (MOELDs)
- We know that the energy levels of MOs are also quantised.
- MOELDs visualise the formation and consequently the qualitative energy levels of MOs relative to the AOs.
- Electrons in MOELDs obey Aufbau, Hund's, and Pauli as they would for AOs.
### 3.1. Drawing MOELDs
- The number of MOs must be the same as the number of AOs.
	- Antibonding orbitals are **destabilised**, hence **higher energy** compared to AOs.
	- Bonding orbitals are stabilised, hence **lower energy** compared to AOs.
- AOs with **similar energy** produce **more stable** MOs.
- AOs with **good overlap** produce **more stable** MOs.
- If the AOs have a large energy difference they will not combine.
- For hydrogen:
	- Two 1s orbitals combine to from 1σ<sub>g</sub> and 1σ<sub>u</sub>* 
	- As 1σ<sub>g</sub> is **lower in energy** (bonding orbital), the two electrons (one from each AO) will move to occupy this MO.
### 3.2. Bond Order
- Bond order arises from Mo diagrams.
- It is equal to
	- $Bond\space order = \frac{1}{2}[n_{electrons}(Bonding)-n_{electrons}(Antibonding)]$
	- H<sub>2</sub> is $\frac{1}{2}(2-0) = 1$ 
	- H<sub>2</sub><sup>+</sup> is $\frac{1}{2}(1-0) = \frac{1}{2}$ 
	- H<sub>2</sub><sup>-</sup> is $\frac{1}{2}(2-1) = \frac{1}{2}$ 
- It correlates to **observable properties**

|                                                | H<sub>2</sub> | H<sub>2</sub><sup>+</sup> |
| ---------------------------------------------- | ------------- | ------------------------- |
| Bon order                                      | 1             | 0.5                       |
| Bond length (pm)                               | 105           | 74                        |
| Bond dissociation energy (kJ mol<sup>-1</sup>) | 436           | 256                       |
|                                                | Stronger bond | Weaker bond               |
## 4. Born-Oppenheimer approximation
- Electrons are **very light** - 1836 times lighter than protons.
- When the nucleus moves, there is a **vibration** associated with the movement.
- Electrons are so light that they can instantaneously move into position **instantaneously**.
- Regardless of the position, the electrons **always arrange to form lowest  configuration of MOs**
- This let's you consider the relationship of the molecule's **total energy with the distance between atoms.**
- From B-O approximation we can generate a **function that gives the energy in terms of distance**.
	- The curve produced by the function is known as '**potential energy surfaces.'**
- The minimum of this function occurs at the **equilibrium bond length**, which is where there is a **balance between bonding and repulsion.**
	- As bond length increases, the bond is increasingly disrupted, weakening the overlap.
	- As bond length decreases, the atoms begin to experience increased Pauli and Coulomb repulsion.'
- B-O approximation also allows the total energy to be seen as a sum of different contributions.
