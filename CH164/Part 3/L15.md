## 2. Modelling reactions
- Modelling chemical reactions effective requires the modelling of how electron distribution changes as bonds are broken then formed.
### 2.1. Collision theory
- Molecules are **dynamic**
- They constantly vibrate and move in space
- Charge-charge repulsion means that all molecules repel each other;
- But collisions can occur, leading to a reaction, if:
	- The molecules are in correct orientations, and
	- they have sufficient energy.
### 2.2. Reactions and redistribution of electrons
- Making and breaking bonds is effectively the process through which reagents are converted into products.
	- This means that the shapes and energies of the orbitals involved change.
#### 2.2.1. Mechanisms and curly arrows
- Curly arrows are used to represent the movement of electron density.
	- Full-headed arrows represent the movement of a whole pair of electrons
	- Half-headed arrows represent the movement of one electron.
#### 2.2.2. Frontier MO (FMO) Interactions
- The most important orbital changes are usually from the HOMO of one reagent and the LUMO of the other. 
	- The newly formed MO usually provides stabilisation that acts as the thermodynamic driving force.
- A reagent that donates electrons from its HOMO is the nucleophile.
- A reagent that donated electrons from its LUMO is the electrophile.
#### 2.2.3. Nucleophiles and electrophiles
- Recall from LCAO that orbitals with closer energy have stronger interactions.
	- So most favourable reaction comes from reagents with LUMO and HOMO of similar energy
	- So nucleophiles with high-lying HOMO and electrophiles low-lying LUMO will have the most favourable reactions.
#### 2.2.4. Charges and steric interactions
- FMO energies are dependent on the overall charge of the molecule.
	- Removing electrons increases Z<sub>eff</sub>, which lowers MO energy, and vv.
	- As a result, anionic species tends to be good nucleophiles, and cationic species tends to be good electrophiles.
- Sterics become more important when discussing large molecules.
	- The reactive functional group can be obstructed by the peripheral groups which do not participate in the reaction.
	- The peripheral groups repel the other reagent, leading to them becoming 'frustrated'.
