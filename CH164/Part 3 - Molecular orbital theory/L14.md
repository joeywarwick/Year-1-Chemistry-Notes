## 2. Conjugation
- Conjugated systems are **systems where π-bonding interactions are present over at least three adjacent atoms.** In organic molecules, this is typically observed as a **planar sequence of multiple sp<sup>2</sup> hybridised atoms.**
	- sp HAOs may not satisfy requirements for conjugation. 
	- Take CH<sub>2</sub>=C=CH<sub>2</sub>, while all can form π-bonds, the central carbon has p-orbitals that are perpendicular to each other
		- So electronic movement between the two π-bonds becomes difficult
	- Hence it is not a conjugated system.
>**NOTE** Conjugated systems may involve more than carbon. Oxygen and nitrogen are common heteroatoms involved in conjugated systems.
- Conjugation stabilises molecules.
### 2.1. 1,3-butadiene
- Traditional Lewis structures do not represent the experimental observations fully.
- π-bonding MOs should be considered.
#### 2.1.1. Disagreements between Lewis structure and experimental observations
- Lewis structure portrays 1,3-butadiene as four carbons connected by alternating single and double bonds.
- There is greater rotational barrier around the central 'single' bond than expected for a C-C bond
- The bond length of the central single bond is longer than expected and the bond lengths of the double bonds are shorter than expected.

| Isolated bond | Length / angstroms |
| ------------- | ------------------ |
| C-C           | 1.54               |
| C=C           | 1.34               |

| 1,3-butadiene bond | Length / angstroms |
| ------------------ | ------------------ |
| C-C                | 1.45               |
| C=C                | 1.37               |

> Recall that bond length is negatively related to strength, and strength is positively related to bond order.
### 2.2. Partial MO scheme
- We can simplify the problem using a 'combination' of HAO and LCAO.
- We know that the carbons are sp<sup>2</sup> hybridised and form σ-bonds. 
- So we only have to consider the p-orbitals which form π-bonds.
- We have four p-orbitals, so we need to deduce the structure of four orbitals.
- We can first break them into two double bonds, each with 2 π-electrons
	- Total 4 π-electrons in the system.
- Each π-orbital can be the result of in-phase or out-of-phase interactions
- So altogether this amounts to four combinations:

| π-orbital 1 | π-orbital 2 | π-orbital 1+2 | Nodes |
| ----------- | ----------- | ------------- | ----- |
| In          | In          | In            | 0     |
| In          | In          | Out           | 1     |
| Out         | Out         | In            | 2     |
| Out         | Out         | Out           | 3     |
#### 2.2.1. Partial MO scheme by graphical methods
- Partial MO scheme can be completed graphically too.
	- First, dummy atoms are added to each end of the chain.
	- Second,. a sine wave is drawn
	- Locations at which the sine wave crosses a chain represents locations at which there is a node.
		- So phase should be inverted at each node.
> **NOTE** When using the graphical method, particularly with an odd number of atoms, if the node falls on an atom, then there is **no** electron density around the given atom.

### 2.3. Electronic structure and UV-visible absorption
- The energy gaps between energy levels are **reduced** with increasing degrees of conjugation.
- So this leads to conjugated molecules being coloured.
## 3. Aromaticity
- Aromaticity is a special case of conjugation where the stability is **greatly enhanced** in some **cyclic conjugated** molecules.
- This also **affects the reactivity** compared to simple and conjugated alkenes.
- Whether or not a cyclic conjugated molecule is aromatic is determined using Huckel's rule: **A continuous system of π-orbitals will be aromatic if it is cyclic, planar, and the number of π-electrons satisfy (4n+2), where n is a positive integer.**
### 3.1. Anti-aromaticity and non-aromatic
- Anti-aromatic molecules have 4n π-electrons but satisfy all other requirements for aromaticity.
- In these molecules the delocalisation of π-electrons is destabilising.
	- This can prevent the molecule from adopting expected geometries, or even formation of the molecule.
- Example is **cyclobutadiene**, where it is cyclic and planar, but has 4 π-electrons.
	- It is very unstable, dimerises quickly into a planar isomer, cyclooctatetraene;
	- The planar isomer then becomes a non-planar 'tub' isomer that is non-aromatic.
		- Non-aromatic is more stable than anti-aromatic.
> **NOTE** Non-aromatic, as seen here, is different from anti-aromatic.
### 3.2. Benzene
- The most famous aromatic molecule is Benzene, frequently seen in many organic chemical structures.
- While Kekule predicted the structure of benzene as a triene ring, X-ray crystallography shows that all bonds in benzene are of the equal length.
- The benzene is also a lot more stable than triene molecules.
#### 3.2.1. NMR spectroscopy of benzene
- The moving electrons along the π-electron ring in benzene creates a magnetic field which opposes the external field.
	- So inside the molecule the magnetic fields are weaker;
	- Outside the molecule the magnetic fields are stronger.
- This leads to a difference in chemical shifts in NMR spectra.
#### 3.2.2 Partial MO scheme of benzene
- Again, we use a hybrid HAO/LCAO approach to construct the MOELD
- Lowest energy is always all in-phase (1 MO)
- Next up is one node, which can either come between atoms or on atoms (2 MOs)
- Next up is two nodes, which can also land on atoms or between (2 MOs).
- Finally is three nodes, which is all out-of-phase (1 MO).
#### 3.2.3. Frost's graphical method
- Frost devised a clever method to work out the energy levels and bonding/antibonding.
1. Draw a circle
2. Draw a regular polygon within the circle that has one corner pointing straight down
3. Each intersection between the circle and the polygon represents one MO
> **NOTE** There can be degenerate MOs
4. A horizontal line through the centre of the circle separates bonding and antibonding MOs.
> **NOTE** Orbitals that sit on the line are **non-bonding**.
5. Fill the MOs.
- Using this method, we can look back at cyclobutadiene.
	- In the ideal D<sub>4h</sub> symmetry, it has one bonding MO, one antibonding MO, and two non-bonding MOs.
	- The bonding MO is populated with a pair
	- The non-bonding MOs each has one unpaired electron.
		- When the non-bonding electrons delocalise the system becomes destabilised.
### 3.3. Polycyclic and heterocyclic compounds
- Consider naphthalene, which consists of two benzene rings fused together.
	- Both the outer large ring (π = 10) and smaller inner ring (π = 6) satisfy Huckel's.
- Heterocyclic compounds can also be aromatic
	- They are the most common type of aromatic compounds
> **NOTE** The heteroatom must also be sp<sup>2</sup> such that it is planar to satisfy Huckel's.
