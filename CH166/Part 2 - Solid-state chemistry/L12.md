## 2. Ionic bonding
- Initial definition is basically attraction between **oppositely-charged ions.**
### 2.1. Energetics of ionic bonding
- Viewing ionic bonding energetically, the energy can be defined as the **lattice enthalpy** ΔH<sub>L</sub><sup>o</sup>.
	- It is the energy required to go from an ionic solid to its constituent ions in the **gaseous** phase:
		- $AB_{(s)} \rightarrow A^+_{(g)} + B^-_{(g)}$ 
	- It is an **endothermic** process.
	- It cannot be measured, but calculated using the **Born-Haber cycle.**
	- The reverse of lattice enthalpy is **lattice formation enthalpy.**
		- $A^+_{(s)} + B^-_{(g)} \rightarrow AB_{(s)}$
		- It is an **exothermic** process.
> **NOTE** The LFE is always exothermic, because the process decreases entropy.
	- We want ΔG to be positive
		- $\Delta G = \Delta H - T\Delta S$
	- So ΔH must be greater than TΔS.
### 2.1.1. Born-Haber cycle
- We start by considering an ionic solid, in this case KCl:
	1. From solid KCl, we first need to consider its formation enthalpy.
		- The **negative of the formation enthalpy** is the energy required to go from crystalline solid to **atoms** in their standard state.
		- $KCl_{(s)} \rightarrow K_{(s)} + \frac{1}{2}Cl_{2(g)}$ 
			- -(-438) kJ/mol = 438 kJ/mol
	2. Next we need to convert **solid** potassium into **gaseous** potassium. 
		- $K_{(s)} \rightarrow K_{(g)}$
			- 89 kJ/mol
	3. Next gaseous potassium **atoms** need to be ionised into gaseous potassium **ions**.
		- $K_{(g)} \rightarrow K^+_{(g)} + e^-$ 
			- 425 kJ/mol
	4. Next we need to convert 0.5 mol of chlorine **molecule** into 1 mol of chlorine **atom.**
		- $\frac{1}{2}Cl_{2(g)} \rightarrow Cl{(g)}$
			- 122 kJ/mol
	5. Next the chlorine will be ionised.
		- $\frac{1}{2}Cl_{2(g)} + e^- \rightarrow Cl^-_{(g)}$
			- -355 kJ/mol
	6. Next we can calculate the LFE and hence the LE.
		- Note that the total energies must sum to **zero**.
			- -719 kJ/mol.
> **NOTE** All of the above are measured at standard conditions.
#### 2.1.2. Estimation of lattice enthalpy
- The Born-Haber process is often difficult and complicated because of the standard conditions required.
- We can **estimate** the lattice enthalpy to simplify the process.
	- We are effectively measuring/estimating the **attraction between oppositely charged ions**.
	- While considering the **repulsion** between **similarly charged** ions.
- A good starting point is the **Coulomb law**:
	- $V_{AB} = \frac{(z_Ae) \times (z_Be)}{4 \pi \epsilon_0 d_{AB}}$
		- z<sub>A</sub>e and z<sub>B</sub>e is the charge between the two oppositely-charged ions.
		- 4π accounts for the fact that they are seen as spheres.
		- ε<sub>0</sub> is the **vacuum permittivity**, or how much electrical conductivity there is in vacuum. It is a **constant**.
		- d<sub>AB</sub> is the distance between the two charge centre (nuclei).
> Essentially this describes the energy present between two ions accounting for their charge and size.
- The Coulomb law per se **is insufficient**. We also need to know the **number of oppositely-charged ions surrounding an ion.**
	- This can be 4, 6, 8 dependent on the structure (See L11)
