### 2.1. Empirical formula for lattice enthalpy
- Compared to the previous equation, 
	- $V_{AB} = \frac{(z_Ae) \times (z_Be)}{4 \pi \epsilon_0 d_{AB}}$
- We can use another equation to model the lattice enthalpy:
	- $\Delta H_L^o = \frac{N_A|z_Az_B|e^2}{4 \pi \epsilon_0 d} (1-\frac{d*}{d})A$
		- This is an **empirical** formula known as the **Born-Mayer** equation.
> Empirical formulas are derived **experimentally**
#### 2.1.1. Madelung constant
- The A in the above equation is the **Madelung constant.**
	- It does not have a unit.
	- In order to work it out we need to consider both forces between oppositely charged and similarly charged ions.
		- For the rock salt structure, there are **six** opposite charges **within distance 1**.
			- $Madelung = \frac{6}{1}$
		- There are 12 permutations for translations for distance 2.
			-  They are **similarly charged** ions, so the are disruptive of the crystal.
				- The distance between two similarly charged ions is $\sqrt{2}$.
			- $Madelung = \frac{6}{1} - \frac{12}{\sqrt{2}}$
		- At 3, there are 8 permutations.
			- They are again oppositely charged ions.
		- $Madelung = \frac{6}{1} - \frac{12}{\sqrt{2}} + \frac{8}{\sqrt{3}}$
	- We can see that the numerator is always 6, 8, or 12, and that the denominator is of increasing distance.
		- So the Madelung constant will **converge towards a number**.
> **NOTE** The value of the Madelung constant, and hence the Coulomb law, is structure specific.
#### 2.1.2. Distance corrections
- The final thing to consider is the $1 - \frac{d*}{d}$ term.
	- When we used the radii as the distance, it is not good because:
		- Coulomb law treats ions a **absolutely positive and negative.**
		- Ions have electrons, so a positive ion will have **negative charges on the surface.**
	- To fix this, we apply a **correction** by using a **constant's ratio against the distance between charges.**
		- d* is the constant. 
- This empirical equation estimates the lattice enthalpy for very ionic compounds, but decreases in accuracy as covalency increases.
	- If we only consider 1+ ions, LiF will have very high charge density
		- CsI is much lower, and hance more covalency.
- We can also see that this empirical equation contains many constants:
	- $\pi$
	- N<sub>A</sub>, Avogadro's
	- $\epsilon_0$, Vacuum permittivity
	- A, Madelung
	- d*
	- e, elementary charge
- We can deduce that the lattice enthalpy is dependent on a few variables:
	- It is **directly proportional** to the charge
	- It is **inversely proportional** to the distance
### 2.2. Solubility
- An obvious application is the solubility.
	- $MX \rightarrow M^+_{(aq)} + X^-_{(aq)}$
- A common observation is that the solubility of salts with ions of **very different** sizes are more soluble.
	- e.g. CsF is a lot more soluble than LiF
- But this does not necessarily follow the lattice enthalpy.
	- CsI's lattice enthalpy is much smaller than LiF.
		- So CsF should be less soluble than CsI, but this is not the case.
- This is because energy is required to overcome the lattice energy.
	- i.e. the **hydration enthalpy**.
- $\Delta H_{hyd} \propto charge / \frac{1}{d}$
	- This is the **same as lattice enthalpy**, but they do not follow the same rules.
		- The **lattice enthalpy** is proportional to the charge and the **inverse** of the distance between the charges.
			- $\Delta H_L \propto \frac{1}{r_++r_-}$
		- The **hydration enthalpy** is proportional to the charge, but it is proportional to the **sum of the inverses** of the charge radius.
			- $\Delta H_{hyd} \propto \frac{1}{r_+} + \frac{1}{r_-}$
	- For an example, let's use an example set of numbers:

| r<sub>+</sub> | r<sub>-</sub> | ΔH<sub>L</sub> | ΔH<sub>hyd</sub> |
| ------------- | ------------- | -------------- | ---------------- |
| 1             | 9             | 0.1            | 1.11             |
| 2             | 8             | 0.1            | 0.625            |
| 3             | 7             | 0.1            | 0.476            |
| 4             | 6             | 0.1            | 0.417            |
| 5             | 5             | 0.1            | 0.4              |
- We can also see from this table that as radius mismatch increases, **more hydration enthalpy is obtained**, hence increasing solubility.
## 3. Stability of carbonates
- Consider calcium carbonate, CaCO<sub>3</sub>, and magnesium carbonate, MgCO<sub>3</sub>.
	- If they are heated sufficiently, they undergoe decomposition to calcium oxide/magnesium oxide and carbon dioxide.
		- $CaCO_3 \rightarrow CaO + CO_2$
		- $MgCO_3 \rightarrow MgO + CO_2$
	- When the decomposition happens, the anion reduces in size, while the cation remains the same size.
		- Because magnesium carbonate was **smaller** than calcium carbonate to begin with, the shrink is **proportionally larger, leading to a greater effect.**
			- Magnesium carbonate gains **more lattice enthalpy** when decomposed.
	- So magnesium reacts first, because it is **thermodynamically more favourable**.
- A similar effect is present in **alkaline metals** too;
	- From lithium carbonate to caesium carbonate, the stability increases, and the thermal decomposition temperature increases.
>  The stability of carbonates is part of the reason why sodium bicarbonate (sodium hydrogen carbonate), NHCO<sub>3</sub> is used in baking. It is the right stability to ensure the cake rises at a reasonable temperature.
