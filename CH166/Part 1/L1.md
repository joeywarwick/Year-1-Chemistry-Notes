## 2. Electronic configurations.
### 2.1. Exchange integral/exchange energy
- Atoms prefer their unpaired electrons to spin in the same direction.
- This is due to the presence of **exchange energy**.
- Take the following two configurations for example:

|                 | 2px | 2py  | 2pz |
| --------------- | --- | ---- | --- |
| Configuration 1 | Up  | Up   |     |
| Configuration 2 | Up  | Down |     |
- Configuration 1 is preferred due to the presence of degenerate representations.
	- That is, the two electrons can swap without changing anything.
### 2.2. Energy of atomic orbitals
- The presence of radial nodes and penetration is the reason behind the order of AO energies.
- 2s is lower than 2p, and this is due to it's ability to penetrate the 2p orbital, causing some of its electron density to be closer to the nucleus.
	- Although some electron density is further away from the nucleus than 2p, overall it is lower in energy than 2p.
- Similarly, 3p is lower than 3d, because 3p has a radial node and can penetrate 3d.
#### 2.2.1. The 3d and 4s orbitals
- The 3d and 4s orbitals are of **approximately equivalent energy**. 
- For potassium and calcium, they are larger than the ensuing elements, so 4s is **lower** than 3d, leading to 4s being filled first.
- For scandium and beyond, the 4s is **higher** than 3d, so 3d is filled first.
	- So the valence electrons are **generally d-electrons** when we discuss transition metals (TMs).
## 3. Transition metal complexes
### 3.1. Structure of a TM complex
- TM complexes typically consists of two species:
	- a metal, often an ion.
	- ligands surrounding the metal
		- Most commonly six, but can be any integer.
> When there are six ligands the ligand is an octahedron.
#### 3.1.1. Inner and outer spheres
- The TM complex can be charged due to two reasons:
	- The metal is an ion, or;
	- The ligand(s) are charged.
- The metal and the ligand is defined as the **inner sphere**.
- To balance the charge, you will typically have a **counterion**.
	- The counterion is the **outer sphere**.
### 3.2. Formation of TM complexes
- Atoms, ions, and molecules can be a ligand if they have **at least one lone pair.** 
	- They can therefore be seen as a base.
> **NOTE** The requirement for a species to be basic is to have one lone pair. 
- The metal, on the other hand, acts like an acid.
- Effectively, the formation of a TM complex is an acid-base reaction.
### 3.3. Nomenclature of TM complexes
- The name follows the format of cation-anion.
#### 3.3.1. Naming the cation
- General rule is **'number-ligand-metal-charge'**.
- For example:
	- [Fe<sup>2+</sup>(H<sub>2</sub>O)<sub>6</SUB>] cation
		- Hexaaquairon (II)
	- [Cr<sup>3+</sup>(NH<sub>3</sub>)<sub>6</SUB>] 
		- Hexaamminechromium (III)
>**NOTE** When the ligand is NH<sub>3</sub>, the name is ammine, not amine.
#### 3.3.2. Naming the anion
- Anions are named in the same way for standard ionic compounds.
#### 3.3.3. Negatively-charged TM complexes
- The same cation-anion rules still apply.
- Take [Fe<sup>2+</sup>(CN<sup>-</sup>)<sub>6</SUB>]<sup>4-</sup> with two magnesium
	- DImagnesium hexacyanoferrate
- Note that instead of iron it's now ferrate.
	- Similarly, instead of manganese it would be manganate;
	- Copper goes to cuprate
### 3.4. Isomers of TM complexes
- There can be more than one species of ligands in a complex.
- Take a hexaligand complex with ligand A and B in ratio 2:4.
	- If the ligand A is on opposite ends, the complex is the **trans** isomer.
	- If they are perpendicular to each other the complex is **cis**.
> **NOTE** These are the only two isomers for these types of complexes, because you can always rotate the complex.
- We can also have complexes where the two ligands are in 3:3 ratio. We can draw two triangles to connect the three ligands of each type.
	- If the triangles **does not** crosses the central metal, the complex is **facial**, or **fac-**.
	- If the triangle **does** cross the central metal, the complex is **meridional**, or **mer-**.
#### 3.4.1. Symmetry
- Symmetry allows us to **predict the properties** of molecules with the same symmetry.
- For the four isomers mentioned above, their point groups are:
- **trans D<sub>4h</sub>**
- **cis C<sub>2v</sub>.**
- **fac** C<sub>3v</sub>
- **mer** C<sub>2v</sub>
