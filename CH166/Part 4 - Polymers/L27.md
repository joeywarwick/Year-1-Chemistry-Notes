## 2. Polymers in the solid state
- There are two types of plastics, if we define them thermally.
	- Thermoplastic, which softens when heated and can be molded;
		- Thermoplastic has chains that are not linked together
		- Common examples include:
			- PP
			- PMMA
			- PS
			- PET
	- Thermosets, which undergo crosslinking through a curing reaction so it cannot soften reversibly.
		- Thermosets have chains that are linked together.
		- Examples include:
			- Bakelite
			- Epoxy resins
	- Elastomers, which are amorphous polymers. They exist at temperatures above their T<sub>g</sub> (see below).
		- They tend to be soft, and sometimes crosslinked.
> **NOTE** Rubber and elastomers are used interchangeably/
### 2.1. Crystallinity
- Polymers in the solid state are very strong if packed regularly, should their stereochemistry permit this (see L26).
- They can crystallise spontaneously if there is a driving factor.
- But there are two important temperatures:
	- Melting point, and
	- Glass transition temperature, notated T<sub>g</sub>, 
- Hydrogen bonding increases crystallinity;
	- The bonding helps the chains to order themselves regularly.
		- So Polyamides like Kevlar tend to be more crystalline than polyesters.
#### 2.1.1. Glass transition temperature
- Glass transition temperature, T<sub>g</sub>, is the temperature at which an amorphous polymer changes from a brittle glassy texture to a rubber.
	- Highly crystalline polymers with good packing properties tend to have a high T<sub>g</sub>.
	- For an elastomer to be useful, we would require a T<sub>g</sub> in the negatives, so it is soft in ambient tempreatures.
> **NOTE** There is no direct relationship between T<sub>g</sub> and the melting point, T<sub>m</sub>.
- Volume of a solid = volume of the molecules (V) + free volume (V<sub>f</sub>).
- At temperatures below T<sub>g</sub>, the fractional free volume, $\frac{V_f}{V+V_f}$ is constant.
- T<sub>g</sub> is a second-order transition, T<sub>m</sub> is first-order transition.
	- So T<sub>g</sub> is a gradual change.
	- T<sub>m</sub> is a qualitative change.
