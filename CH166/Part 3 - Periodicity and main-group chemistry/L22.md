## 2. Group 17 elements
- AKA halogens
- Most halogens exist as molecular diatomics:
	- F<sub>2</sub>
	- Cl<sub>2</sub>
	- Br<sub>2</sub>
- Heavy halogens exist as semi-metallic solid:
	- As
- Electronic configuration ns<sup>2</sup> np<sup>5</sup>.
	- Group oxidation state +7
- F and Cl can form +7, but it is difficult for Br-As, due to high Z<sub>eff</sub>.
	- Forming -1 by adding one electron is however very favourable.
### 2.1. Physical properties of halogens
- The melting points increase down the group.
	- So at RTP:
		- F and Cl are gases
		- Br is a liquid
		- I is a solid.
- We expect shorter bonds to be stronger:
	- This holds for Cl-Cl to I-I.
	- But F-F is anomalous.
		- It's bond enthalpy is very low despite being very short.
			- This is because the electrons repel strongly when they are very close to each other, which disrupts the bond.
- F<sub>2</sub> is also very reducing; it would like to be oxidised to 1-. The reducing power decreases down the group.
	- This is shown by the highly positive electrode potential for $F_2 \rightarrow F^-$
	- As well as the F-F bond being very weak.
	- Hydration enthalpy of F is also very large.
### 2.2. Hydrogen halides
- Hydrogen will from halides with all of the halogens in the simple HX format.
- There is no stabilisation whatsoever, so the trend is quite simple and linear.
	- Dissociation enthalpy decreases
	- pK<sub>a</sub> decreases, acid strength increases.
		- This is because H-I is a weaker bond than H-F.
	- H-F boils at a high temperature, while the others boil below 0 degrees.
		- This is due to hydrogen bonding between H-F molecules.
### 2.3. Halogen oxyacids
- As expected, they form oxyacids with oxidation states +1, 3, 5, and 7.
	- Many are unstable outside of solutions.
- Pauling rule still holds;
	- For perchloric acid, HOClO<sub>3</sub>, 1 hydroxy 3 oxy, so very strong acid, pK<sub>a</sub> around -7.
#### 2.3.1. Perbromic acid
- Perbromic acid, HBrO<sub>4</sub> is known as the 'non-existent compound'.
- Was made using radiochemistry:
	- SeO<sub>4</sub> was made, then allowed to decay to BrO<sub>4</sub>, which then decayed into Kr + O<sub>2</sub>.
- Later made with fluorine
	- $BrO_3 + F_2 + 2OH^- \rightarrow BrO_4 + 2F^- + H_2O$
		- This had ~20% yield.
> The highest +7 oxidation state is very unstable, particularly after transition metals. The high Z<sub>eff</sub> makes reduction desirable.
#### 2.3.2. Periodic acid (Per-iodic acid, not periodic)
- Iodine is larger, so it can support higher CN. Again it forms dimers by sharing 2 or 3 oxygens.
	- Instead of HOIO<sub>3</sub>, it forms H<sub>6</sub>I<sub>2</sub>O<sub>10</sub>.
### 2.4. Interhalogen compounds
- Halogens can form compounds between themselves.
- The charged anions formed are usually quite large.
	- As a result, they will prefer **large cations**.
- Large cations and large anions generate stronger bonds. (See L16)
#### 2.4.1. Polyiodides
- Iodine is not very soluble in water, but is very soluble in organic solvents.
- This changes when it is converted to a polyiodide ion, for example, triiodide:
	- $I_2 + I^- \rightarrow I_3^-$
		- The triiodide consists of three iodines in a chain, I-I-I.
- This is very large, so again, it prefers large cations.
	- The I-I bond length in these salts are around 2.90 A (varies dep on cation), so is not very strongly bound.
> There are many different polyiiodides possible, such as I<sub>42</sub><sup>-</sup>. The longest straight chain is I<sub>16</sub>, and very large cations are required.
