## 2. Group 18 elements
- AKA Noble gases
- Electron configuration ns<sup>2</sup> np<sup>6</sup>
	- Group oxidation state +8
		- Closed shell, so a lot of energy required to remove electrons.
### 2.1. Physical properties
- They have very low melting and boiling points.
	- Helium can never form a solid, the intermolecular forces are too weak.
- Ionisation energies very high, because Z<sub>eff</sub> very high.
- Electron affinity also low, because it requires adding into a new shell.
### 2.2. Noble gas clathrates
- Noble gases are the correct size to fill the holes in ice.
- Ice forms a pseudo-tetrahedral structure:
	- 4 O-H bonds, 2 bonded 2 hydrogen bonding.
- They are not really compounds, as there is no transfer of electrons.
#### 2.2.1. C<sub>60</sub>
- C<sub>60</sub> can trap noble gases inside the carbon cage.
### 2.3. Noble gas compounds
- The first noble gas compound was discovered in 1960.
	- O<sub>2</sub> reacts with PtF<sub>6</sub> to from [O<sub>2</sub><sup>+</sup>] [PtF<sub>6</sub>]
	- Because the first IE of O<sub>2</sub> is actually higher than Xe, it can oxidise Xe.
		- $Xe + PtF_6 \rightarrow XePtF_6$
> **NOTE** Radon chemistry has not been extensively studied due to limited availability of Rn.
- Xenon was also able to react directly with fluorine:
	- $Xe + F_2 \rightarrow XeF_2$
	- $Xe + 2F_2 \rightarrow XeF_4$
	- $Xe + 3F_2 \rightarrow XeF_6$
		- They require increasingly harsh conditions
#### 2.3.1. Structure of Xe fluorides
- XeF<sub>2</sub> exist as layers intermolecularly bonded between the fluorines.
- XeF<sub>4</sub> exists as square planar molecules.
- XeF<sub>6</sub> exists as distorted octahedron.
#### 2.3.2. XeF<sub>6</sub>
- The actual structure of XeF<sub>6</sub> is actually only slightly distorted from a octahedron.
### 2.4. Xenon fluoride ions
#### 2.4.1. Xenon fluoride anions
- In the high oxidation states, you can make anions with Xe.
	- [XeF<sub>7</sub>]<sup>-</sup>
		- A capped distorted octahedron
	- [XeF<sub>6</sub>]<sup>2-</sup>
		- A square antiprism
#### 2.4.2. Xenon fluoride cations
- With very strong fluoride acceptors Xe<sub>2</sub><sup>2+</sup> can form from XeF<sup>+</sup>.
	- Xe<sub>2</sub><sup>2+</sup> is very easy to fall apart, its Xe-Xe bonds are longer than 3A.
