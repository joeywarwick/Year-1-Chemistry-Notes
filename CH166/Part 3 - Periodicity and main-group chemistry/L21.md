## 2. Group 16 elements
- AKA Chalcogens
- Electron configuration ns<sup>2</sup> np<sup>4</sup>
- Group oxidation state +6
	- +4 also possible for Se onwards
	- -2 also quite favourable
		- Increased electron affinity
### 2.1. Physical properties
- Oxygen exists as O<sub>2</sub> and O<sub>3</sub> (ozone). 
	- Ozone is quite oxidising and likes to be reduced.
	- Oxygen's O=O double bonds are stronger than 2x O-O single bonds.
		- Strong pi-bonds
> **NOTE** As illustrated with group 15, S=S < 2x S-S due to weak pi-bonding.
- Their 1st electron affinity is always exothermic, so it is always favourable to add an electron.
	- The second EA is not favourable, as adding electron to negatively charged species is difficult.
> So the existence of sulfide and oxide must be due to the presence of strong bonds.
### 2.2. Allotropes of sulfur
- Sulfur has more allotropes than any other element
- Only contains single bonds
	- The most stable form is the a-form, S<sub>8</sub>.
	- S<sub>6-7</sub> is also possible.
	- Chains of sulfur atoms are also possible
#### 2.3.1. Structure of Se and Te
- Selenium has many allotropes like sulfur
- Tellurium has one structure that consists of many spiral chains closely packed together.
### 2.3. Oxides and sulfides
#### 2.3.1. Ionic oxides
- Ionic oxides like MgO have a rock-salt like structure, similar to NaCl.
- You can also form other anions with oxygen apart from O<sup>2-</sup>:
	- O<sub>2</sub><sup>-</sup>, superoxides;
	- O<sub>2</sub><sup>2-</sup>, peroxides.
#### 2.3.2. Covalent oxides
- Covalent oxides like CO, CO<sub>2</sub>, and P<sub>4</sub>O<sub>10</sub> are also possible.
- Besides gaseous molecular structure, giant structures like SiO<sub>2</sub> are also possible.
#### 2.3.3. Sulfides
- Sulfides form in a similar manner to oxides:
	- S<sup>2-</sup>, Sulfide
	- S<sub>2</sub><sup>2-</sup>, Persulfide
> **NOTE** No supersulfide due to sulfide pi-bonding being weak and unstable.
- They often have **layered** structures instead of close-packed structures.
	- Molybdenum sulfide, MoS<sub>2</sub>, is made of many 3 atom-thick layers.
- Persulfides, compared to peroxides, tend to be quite stable.
	- The S-S single bond is quite strong.
	- No entropy gain by decomposing - no S<sub>2</sub> gas
- Sodium reacts with S<sub>8</sub> to give Na<sub>2</sub>S<sub>4</sub>.
	- This form the S<sub>4</sub><sup>2-</sup> ion, which is a fragment of the larger S<sub>8</sub>.
	- S-S bonds are sufficiently strong to support this.
### 2.4. Group 16 halides
#### 2.4.1. Oxygen halides
- Fluoride is more electronegative than oxygen, so it exists as fluoride.
- It can form OF<sub>2</sub> with oxygen.
	- This is structurally similar to water.
- It can also from O<sub>2</sub>F<sub>2</sub>.
	- O-O is much stronger than  O-F, so it will dissociate into F-O=O<sup>+</sub> and F<sup>-</sup> readily.
- All the other halogens are less electronegative than oxygen, so they will all have O<sup>2-</sup> anion.
#### 2.4.2. Sulfur halides
- Sulfur can form a wide range of halides around a range of oxidation states.
- We expect even number oxidation states to be possible, due to these resulting in even numbers of valence electrons.
	- +2, +4, +6.
- There are also odd oxidation states:
	- <+1, +1, +5.
	- They all contain S-S bonds as dimers or chains.
	- This prevents the reactivity associated with a free radical species.
#### 2.4.3. Hypervalency in SF<sub>6</sub>
- The MOELD shows that only 4 bonding pairs.
	- So bond order 2/3.
- Bonds are quite weak and SF<sub>6</sub> is reactive.
- OF<sub>6</sub> is not possible because it's too small.
	- Like NF<sub>5</sub>, it cannot support such a high coordination number.
#### 2.4.4. Selenium and tellurium halides
- Se and Te forms TeF<sub>4</sub> and SeCl<sub>4</sub>. 
	- They size of Te and Se require more ligand atoms.
		- TeF<sub>4</sub> has 5 fluorines around it, but the F is shared between two Te.
		- SeCl<sub>4</sub> has octahedral Se, but the Cl is again shared between Se.
- They also form Te<sub>3</sub>Cl<sub>2</sub> and TeI.
	- Tellurium forms Te-Te chains
		- This is similar to metallic form
### 2.5. Oxyacids of heavy chalcogens
- Pauling's rule (See L20) states that: For oxyacids XO<sub>a</sub>(OH)<sub>b</sub>:
	- **pK<sub>a</sub> for oxyacids are estimated to be 8 - 5a.**
		- So the central atom, whether it's S, Se, or Te, has no effect on acid strength.
- On this basis, because of stabilisation from delocalisation, sulfuric acid is much stronger than telluric acid.
> **NOTE** This does not mean that sulfuric acid is a better oxidising agent.
- Telluric acid is very unstable:
	- Inert pair, so it really wants to reduce to 3+.
### 2.6. Hydrides
- Predictably, as we go down the group the bonds get weaker and longer.
- The pK<sub>a</sub> also decreases down the group, indicating stronger acids.
- When the hydrides are put into water, they deprotonate.
	- If we considered the stability:
		- Anion should be less stable in smaller anions, because there is no way to delocalise the charge.
		- We expect smaller ions to have higher hydration enthalpy.
		- So we would expect water to be a stronger acid than telluric acid, which isn't the case.
- We can therefore say that it's the E-H bond strength that determines the pK<sub>a</sub>.
	- The weaker the bond the easier it dissociates.
