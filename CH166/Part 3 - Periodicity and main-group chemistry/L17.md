## 2. Hydrogen
- Hydrogen is the simplest element, and we can consider it as either s- or p-block.
	- It can form either ionic or covalent compounds.
	- In terms of oxidation states
		- In water and methane, hydrogen is +1.
		- In NaH or SbH<sub>3</sub>, hydrogen is -1.
### 2.1. Interstitial hydrides
- In metals like palladium, when it reacts with hydrogen gas:
	- Hydrogen diffuses into the metallic structure, but the metallic structure does not change.
	- Bu formally the hydrogen is still -1, because Pd's electronegativity is lower.
> These compounds are used as a way to capture and store hydrogen for applications such as fuel.
## 2. Group 1/2 elements
### 2.1. Electron configuration
- Group 1 elements:
	- Have configuration ns<sup>1</sup>
		- This electron is only **loosely** bound to the nucleus, so ionised easily.
		- They will only have +1 oxidation state.
- Group 2 elements:
	- Have configuration ns<sup>2</sup>
		- These electrons are also rather easily to remove.
			- So they will be rather reactive.
		- They will have 2+ oxidation state.
> Group 1 elements have the lowest Z<sub>eff</sup> and hence the lowest ionisation energy.
- The ionisation energy decreases in Group 1 from lithium to caesium, but increases again from caesium to francium.
	- This is due to the extra lanthanide series present, giving it extra Z<sub>eff</sub>
### 2.2. Oxides of group 1
- When alkali metals are burnt in excess oxygen, they form oxides.
- Lithium forms the oxide:
	- $2Li + O_2 \rightarrow Li_2O$
> **NOTE** In air **liithium** reacts to give lithium nitride: $6Li + N_2 \rightarrow 2Li_3N$
	- This is because lithium has very high charge density and is strongly polarisable.
- Sodium forms the **peroxide**:
	- $2Na + O_2 \rightarrow Na_2O_2$
- Potassium, rubidium, and caesium all form the **superoxide**:
	- $K + O_2 \rightarrow KO_2$
#### 2.2.1. Stability of group 1 per/superoxides.
- The stability of group 1 peroxides and superoxides can be assessed using a thermodynamic cycle.
	- Essentially, the stability is dependent on the lattice enthalpy
	- This is dependent on the charge and radii
		- The charge is always 1, so we only need to consider the oxides' radii.
- Radius of oxide is smaller than peroxide, so lattice enthalpy of oxide is larger than peroxide.
	- Because LE is inversely proportional to sum of cation and anion radii, if the metal ion is large there is **little driving force** for decomposition.
	- Recall that **compounds with like ions are more favourable**, hence the stabilisation from the large cation to the anion is greater, so it does not decompose.
### 2.3. Solution chemistry of group 1 cations
- The charge densities are quite low, so complexation is poor
- No valence d-orbitals, so few varieties of bonding available
- 'Hard' cations want to complex with 'hard' ligands.
> **NOTE** Hard ligands are not the same as strong-field ligands. They typically have oxygen atoms.
- So they only complex with particular cyclic ligands with multiple bonding points that simultaneously bond to the ion. 
	- For example, 18-crown-6 has a ring that is the perfect size for potassium to fit into. It's six oxygens simultaneously bond to the potassium.
> These ligands are therefore selective and have orders of preference in complexing.
### 2.4. Covalency in group 1/2 compounds
#### 2.4.1. Covalency in group 1 compounds
- **Only lithium** can form covalent compounds due to high charge density.
- Examples include methyl lithium, CH<sub>3</sub>Li:
	- This appears to consist of CH<sub>3</sub><sup>-</sup> and Li<sup>+</sup>, but in fact it forms a cubic tetramer.
#### 2.4.2. Covalency in group 2 compounds
- Group 2 has higher charge density than their group 1 counterparts, so elements like beryllium will form covalent compounds frequently.
	- Be(CH<sub>3</sub>)<sub>2</sub>
	- BeH<sub>2</sub>, which is giant covalent instead of ionic.
- Magnesium also forms covalent compounds to a certain extent:
	- Grignard reagents (RMgX), where R is an alkyl group and X is a halide.
### 2.5. Anomalies of Be
- Be is very small and has a very high charge density.
- Apart from covalency, it:
	- Forms amphoteric compounds (can act as base or acid), such as Be(OH)<sub>2</sub>:
		- It can react with acid to form a tetraaquacomplex.
		- It can react with base to form as tetrahydroxycomplex.
	- Has a low coordination number:
		- BeO is tetrahedral metal
		- MgO and onwards is rock salt structure.
> This is sensible; smaller molecules only allow for smaller coordination number.
