## 2. Properties of atoms and ions I
- We will recap on the properties of atoms and ions covered in A Levels and CH164.
### 2.1. Atomic size
- For **elemental** substances, the atomic size can be simply defined as half of the **interatomic distance**.
- There is a periodicity with atomic radii.
	- The **Group 1** elements are the largest, and the size decreases to the right.
		- This is because Group 1 elements have the lowest **effective nuclear charge**.
		- So as Z<sub>eff</sub> increases, the atom contracts
	- An exception is the **lanthanides and actinides**, although the overall trend still holds.
- Atomic radii also **increases down the group**.
	- Z<sub>eff</sub> decreases due to shielding.
	- This increase is however, **not linear**.
> Many trends are not 'smooth'.
#### 2.2.1. Electronic configurations.
- The configurations generally follow the Aufbau, Hund, and Pauli. However, exceptions exist:
	- Cr is [Ar] 4s<sup>1</sup> 3d<sup>5</sup>
		- This configuration avoids electron pairing.
> This tells us that 4s and 3d are very similar in energy.
- So 4s is lower than 3d towards the left, about the same as 3d in the middle, and higher than 3d towards the right.
- The increase in atomic radii from B to Al is **much smaller** than from Al to Ga. 
	- This is because of the **extra protons from the transition metals** found in Ga, and
	- The valence electrons being from an **inner-field orbital.**\
- This actually explains the exceptions that arise from lanthanides.
	- Hafnium is actually smaller than zirconium, due to the extra attraction from the 14 f-electrons.
	- This effect is called the **lanthanide contraction**.
### 2.3. Ionic radii
- If the two ions are the same, the radius is just half of the radius.
	- Due to presence of two or more types of ions, the definition is more complicated.
		- Electron density **never drops to zero** between ions.
	- To find the radii, x-ray crystallography **is** used.
- The ionic radii also changes, depending on the crystal structure. Hence the experimental data is **averaged**.
> The ions can stretch and contract, like squishy balls.
- So the ionic radii is **not fixed**, it varies from compound to compound.
	- Radii of Na<sup>+</sup> increases from tetrahedral to octahedral to cubic
#### 2.3.1. Thermochemical calculation of ionic radii
- Using the Born-Mayer equation (see L13),  we can calculate the lattice enthalpies.
- But we can also use this to calculate the**radii of ions** by calculating the distance between ions.
	- This is helpful because some ions are not spherical.
- We can use the experimental and theoretical lattice enthalpies to find the degree of covalency and hence the polarisability of ions.
	- LiF:
		- Very small and very high charge density
		- Theoretical is almost the same as experimental
	- LiI:
		- Larger anion, more polarisable
		- Slight deviation between theoretical and experimental.
	- CsI:
		- Cs less polarising
		- Experimental **much higher** than theoretical.
#### 2.3.2. Hardness of ions
> Ions with a large charge density are called **hard ions**. Conversely large and polarisable ions are **soft ions**.
- **Like ions give favourable compounds**.
	- Compared to LiI and CsF, LiF and CsI are more desirable.
#### 2.3.3. Trends in ionic radii
- Similar trend to atoms - ionic radii decreases across the period table, however:
	- Until d<sup>6</sup>, as t<sub>2g</sub> is filled, the radius decreases.
	- After d<sup>6</sup>, the e<sub>g</sub> is filled and the radius increases.
