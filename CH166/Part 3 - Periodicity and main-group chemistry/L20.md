## 2. Group 15 elements
- Their physical properties vary greatly.
	- N, P, and As are nonmetals
		- N is a gas at RTP
		- P and As are solids at RTP.
	- Sb, Bi are metals.
- They exhibit a similar trend to group 14;
	- Pi bonding becomes less effective down the group.
		- N-N triple bond is stronger than 3x N-N single bonds, because overlap is very good.
		- P-P triple bond is weaker than 3x P-P single bonds, because overlap is poor.
			- N prefers to exist as N<sub>2</sub>
			- P prefers to from extended covalent structures.
- MP and BP increases down the group.
### 2.1. Electron configuration
- The atoms take configuration ns<sup>2</sub> np<sup>3</sup>.
	- Group oxidation state +5
	- +3 possible for As, Sb, Bi from inert pair
	- -3 possible with electropositive ions.
### 2.2. Allotropes of phosphorus
> All allotropes contain 3 bonds per phosphorus atom.
- Two main types:
	- White phosphorus:
		- P<sub>4</sub> in molecular forms.
		- Spontaneously combusts in air.
		- Very stable, event at 1800 degrees only ~50% dissociates into P<sub>2</sub>.
	- Red phosphorus:
		- Polymeric chain of P<sub>4</sub>.
		- Different crystalline and glassy forms.
		- Reacts very easily and often violently with oxygen.
			- Strong P-O bonds, P=O bonds also possible.
### 2.3. Nitrides, phosphides, and arsenides
- Pairing with electropositive metals can result in ionic compounds with group 14 ions acting as anions.
- Nitrogen forms two main ions:
	- N<sup>3-</sup>, nitrides;
	- N<sub>3</sub><sup>-</sub>, azides ([N=N=N]<sup>-</sup>)
		- Azides are unstable;
			- They decompose to form nitrogen gas, with increases entropy greatly.
				- $3NaN_3 \rightarrow Na_3N + 4N_2$
- Phosphorus forms just phosphide, because no pi-bonding is possible:
	- CoP<sub>3</sub>, 
	- Co<sub>4</sub>(P<sub>4</sub>)<sub>3</sub>
- Arsenides are somewhat metallic, so they form **alloy** like structures.
	- Na<sub>3</sub>As
### 2.4. Halides of group 15

| N                          | P                             | As                         | Sb                         | Bi              |
| -------------------------- | ----------------------------- | -------------------------- | -------------------------- | --------------- |
| NX<sub>3</sub>             | PX<sub>3</sub>                | AsX<sub>3</sub>            | SbX<sub>3</sub>            | BiX<sub>3</sub> |
| N<sub>2</sub>X<sub>4</sub> | P<sub>2</sub>X<sub>4</sub>    |                            |                            |                 |
|                            | PX<sub>5</sub>, X = F, Cl, Br | AsX<sub>5</sub>, X = F, Cl | SbX<sub>5</sub>, X = F, Cl | BiF<sub>5</sub> |
| N<sub>2</sub>F<sub>2</sub> |                               |                            |                            |                 |
- Nitrogen can form 1+ and 2+ as N<sub>2</sub>O<sub>4</sub>.
	- Odd electrons are unstable
	- Nitrogen stabilises with extra bond.
- They are all capable of forming 3+.
	- Inert pair
	- Can only fit three around 
- P-Bi forms +5 with F-Br, a trigonal bipyramidal.
	- I is not possible because it is too big.
	- Nitrogen cannot form pentahalides.
		- Nitrogen is too small to fit any halides around it.
#### 2.4.1. Stability of group 15 pentachlorides

|     | MCl<sub>5</sub>  | Stability                  |
| --- | ---------------- | -------------------------- |
| N   | NCl<sub>5</sub>  | Not known                  |
| P   | PCl<sub>5</sub>  | Stable                     |
| As  | AsCl<sub>5</sub> | Stable below -50 degrees   |
| Sb  | SbCl<sub>5</sub> | Stable at room temperature |
| Bi  | BiCl<sub>5</sub> | Not known                  |
- N:
	- No pentachloride, too small
- P:
	- Stable.
		- Exists as PCl<sub>4</sub><sup>+</sup> and PCl<sub>6</sub><sup>+</sup> in solid form
- As:
	- Unstable above -50 degrees
		- Inert pair, so reduced easily at +5
- Sb
	- Stable
- Bi:
	- Large atom, 6s strongly bound (inert pair)
	- Bi-Cl bond too weak to compensate for ionisation energy.
- The halides will gradually move from a molecular structure like the trigonal bipyramidal in PCl<sub>5</sub> to a close packed structure in SbF<sub>5</sub> with tetrametric Sb<sub>4</sub>F<sub>20</sub> molecules.
### 2.5. Group 15 oxyacids
- Oxyacids are acids that contain acidic OH groups.
#### 2.5.1. Nitrogen

| Acid                                    | Oxidation state |
| --------------------------------------- | --------------- |
| H<sub>2</sub>N<sub>2</sub>O<sub>2</sub> | +1              |
| HNO<sub>2</sub>                         | +3              |
| HNO<sub>3</sub>                         | +5              |
- The reason N<sup>5+</sub> is present in HNO<sub>3</sub> is due to the presence of pi-bonding between the nitrogen and the oxygen. They give extra strength.
	- Nonetheless this is highly oxidising and it is reduced quite easily.
#### 2.5.2. Phosphorus
- Phosphorus is considerably larger than N, so all of its oxyacids can arrange quite easily in a tetrahedral structure.
- Most common phosphorus oxyacid seen is H<sub>3</sub>PO<sub>4</sub>, and it has a orthophosphoric structure
- Another oxyacid is H<sub>3</sub>PO<sub>3</sub>.
	- While this acid has a P-H bond, an O-H bond is broken regardless when it dissociates.
#### 2.5.3. Pauling's rule for oxyacids
- For oxyacids XO<sub>a</sub>(OH)<sub>b</sub>:
	- $XO_a(OH)_b + H_2O \rightleftharpoons [XO_a(OH)_{b-1}O]^-+ H_3O^+$
- pK<sub>a</sub> for oxyacids are estimated to be 8 - 5a.
	- The more stable the conjugate base is, the greater degree of dissociation, hence stronger acid.
		- Resonance stabilisation
- The pK<sub>a</sub> also increases for each proton dissociation, as removing a proton from an already negatively charged species is diffucult.
