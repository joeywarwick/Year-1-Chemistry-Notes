## 2. Spectrochemical series II
- From the last lecture, we knew that the electron from the ligands had the greatest contribution to stabilisation.
- We can classify ligands as **weak sigma donors**, or **strong sigma donors**.
- Strong sigma donors:
	- Examples:
		- Hydride, the strongest base
		- Ammonia
			- Nitrogen-based compounds are stronger donors with EDGs, like trimethylamine.
		- Phosphorus-based compounds, like PH<sub>3</sub>
	- They do not affect t<sub>2g</sub> as they are non-bonding.
	- For the antibonding e<sub>g</sub>, strong sigma donor leads to strong bonds.
		- Which means that there is more overlap.
			- So electrons populating bonding orbitals go down;
			- But the e<sub>g</sub> goes up.
			- So increases Δ<sub>oct</sub>.
- But strong sigma donors do not explain the spectrochemical series fully.
	- Cyanides, carbon monoxides are all not as strong sigma donors as phosphine and amines.
	- Some ligands which are very strong sigma donors are weak-field ligands.
### 2.1. π-bonding in complexes
- So far we have only considered σ-bonds.
#### 2.1.1. π-donors
- In an octahedral complex, only the t<sub>2g</sub> orbitals can form π-bonds.
	- Where the ligands have p-orbitals and more than one lone pair, it can form π-bonds with the t<sub>2g</sub> orbitals.
	- They are called **π-donors.**
- Examples of π-donors include:
	- Water
	- Halides
	- Sulphur-based
- Not all ligands can form π-bonds:
	- Hydride only has s-orbitals
	- Ammonia's lone pair is already used for a σ-bond.
- π-bonding does not lead to the formation of newer orbitals.
	- The π-electrons still largely belong to the ligand.
	- π-bonding is a very minor effect.
		- The main significance is that t<sub>2g</sub>, which was non-bonding, is now slightly antibonding.
			- This increases its energy
			- So decreases energy gap betweem e<sub>g</sub> and t<sub>2g</sub>.
			- So Δ<sub>oct</sub> smaller.
> **NOTE** When constructing MOELD, these electrons should be left on the ligand side.
- So we will notice that **weak-field ligands tend to be π-donors.**
	- So weak-field ligands do not necessarily mean weaker bonds - the π-bonding makes their bond overall slightly stronger.
#### 2.1.2. π-acceptors
- This still does not explain why carbon monoxide and cyanide are such strong ligands.
	- Both ligands contain triple bonds, which means that there is π-bonding.
- When a MOELD is constructed for both, the π-antibonding orbitals are **not occupied**.
- They are the **correct symmetry to overlap with t<sub>2g</sub> orbitals** from the metal.
- Once they overlap, the t<sub>2g</sub> are the electron donors.
	- This stabilises the t<sub>2g</sub> further, increasing Δ<sub>oct</sub>
- These ligands are therefore **π-acceptors**.
	- **Strong-field ligands tend to be π-acceptors.**
### 2.2. Revisiting the spectrochemical series
- So we can see that π-donors tend to be weak-field (halides, sulphur-based compounds);
- π-neutral tends to be in between (ammonia, hydrides);
- π-acceptors tend to be strong-field (cyanide, carbon monoxide).
