## 2. d-orbitals
### 2.1. Quantum numbers
- Recall from CH164 (L8) that orbitals are characterised by three quantum numbers:
	- Principal, n, tells us about the shell the orbital it is in.
	- Angular momentum, l, tells us about the shape of the orbital. It tells us how many times the orbital 'splits' at the nucleus
	- Magnetic, m<sub>l</sub>, linked to l, tells us the number of orbitals.
- A d-orbital **always has four lobes**, because the l = 2, meaning that the d-orbital splits twice.
	- 3d **only has the angular nodes, no radial nodes.**
	- But **4d will have one radial, 5d two,** and so on.
> Recall from CH164 L8 that number of radial nodes are given by n - l - 1. For 3d this is 3-2-1 = 0 radial nodes.
- d-orbitals are g (as in gerader, see CH164 L11). The opposite of any point in d-orbitals are in the same phase.
	- p-orbitals are u (ungerader).
> A pattern is that where l is even you will have g orbitals, and where l is odd you will have u orbitals.
### 2.2. Directions
- The five d-orbitals are given unique tags:
	- d<sub>xy</sub>
	- d<sub>xz</sub>
	- d<sub>yz</sub>
	- d<sub>x<sup>2</sup>-y<sup>2</sup></sub> 
	- d<sub>z<sup>2</sup></sub> 
- These tags are not arbitrary, we can group them into two groups:

| With square                             | Without square |
| --------------------------------------- | -------------- |
| d<sub>x<sup>2</sup>-y<sup>2</sup></sub> | d<sub>xy</sub> |
| d<sub>z<sup>2</sup></sub>               | d<sub>xz</sub> |
|                                         | d<sub>yz</sub> |
- The d-orbitals **without square** have their lobes pointing **between the axis.** 
	- For example, xy means that the lobes point between the x- and y-axis.
- The d-orbitals **with square** have their lobes **pointing at axis.**
	- d<sub>z<sup>2</sup></sub> has all density pointing at the z-axis
	- d<sub>x<sup>2</sup>-y<sup>2</sup></sub> has two lobes in the direction of y and two in the direction of x.
- Alternatively, the names refer to the **symmetry axis.**
### 2.3. Filling the d-orbitals
- Most transition metals fill up as anticipated, but some fill up oddly.
	- Chromium is 3d5 4s1
		- Chromium takes 3d5 because of exchange energy (See L1). The 5 electrons produces 120 degenerate representations that massively stabilise the atom.
	- Vanadium is 3d3 4s2, but niobium is 4d4 5s1
	- Nickel is 3d8 4s2, but palladium is 4d10
- It is also observed that when TMs are ionised, **s-electrons are removed first.**
	- This is possible due to the energy levels **changing**. 
	- When the d-orbitals are populated their energy levels begin to fluctuate.
- The periodic table representation is true for **isolated metal atoms.**
	- This is rarely the case in practical applications; Metals are commonly found as a part of complexes, ions, and compounds.
- For ions, complexes, and compounds, **d-orbitals are lower in energy than s-orbitals.**
	- **So all valence electrons are d-electrons.**
## 3. Crystal Field Theory (CFT)
- CFT is a simple model for explaining, and therefore predicting, the stability of complexes, using an octahedral model.
- Consider the d-orbitals of a metal atom/ion.
	- They are located at the 'edge' of the atom and are negatively charged.
- Overall the complex may have a positive/neutral charge.
	- Irrelevant, as only the 'friction' between the orbitals of the atom and the ligands are considered.
- We can consider the ligands as **points with negative charges.**
	- So all we need to consider in the **repulsion between the negatively charged ligand 'points' and the metal.**
- The greater the interaction, the greater the d-orbitals will be destabilised.
	- **The further the point charges are from the d-orbitals, the greater the degree of interaction.**
- If we placed a metal ion in water, it will be **surrounded by ligands, so it increases in energy as it gets destabilised by water.**
- The water then surrounds the orbitals as a **sphere.**
- If they were to be arranged as an octahedron, the **degree of destabilisation will depend on the orbital.**
	- As orbitals point in different directions.
- The orbitals which **point directly towards the ligands will be more affected.**
	- They will be more destabilised than the others.
- The orbitals which **point between the axis are less destabilised.**
- As a result the two d-orbitals which point at the axis will increase in energy, and the three which point between will decrease in energy.

| Increase in energy                      | Decrease in energy |
| --------------------------------------- | ------------------ |
| d<sub>x<sup>2</sup>-y<sup>2</sup></sub> | d<sub>xy</sub>     |
| d<sub>z<sup>2</sup></sub>               | d<sub>xz</sub>     |
|                                         | d<sub>yz</sub>     |

- We can assign symmetry labels to these two groups too:

| e<sub>g</sub>                           | t<sub>2g</sub> |
| --------------------------------------- | -------------- |
| d<sub>x<sup>2</sup>-y<sup>2</sup></sub> | d<sub>xy</sub> |
| d<sub>z<sup>2</sup></sub>               | d<sub>xz</sub> |
|                                         | d<sub>yz</sub> |
- The original energy of the d-orbitals (**barycenter**) are not half-way between the energies of e<sub>g</sub> and t<sub>2g</sub>.
	- As two orbitals were destabilised and three were stabilised;
	- The energy of e<sub>g</sub> is $\frac{3}{5} \Delta_o$
	- The energy of t<sub>2g</sub> is  $\frac{2}{5} \Delta_o$
- Overall the system's energy has not changed.
> **Δ<sub>o</sub>** is not 'delta zero'. It is the **crystal-field splitting parameter**, and is read **delta octahedral**.
### 3.1. Evidence for CFT
- Complexes adopt the octahedral shape due to stabilisation of electrons as they move from the original d-orbitals into the stabilised t<sub>2g</sub>.
- Take Hexaaquatitanium (III), [Ti(OH<sub>2</sub>)<sub>6</sub>]<sup>3+</sup>;
	- Titanium ion is 3d1.
	- We can confirm that the splitting occurs by **exciting the electron.**
	- We can measure how much energy is required for the electron to move from ground-state to excited state.
		- This is done using **UV-Vis spectroscopy.**
### 3.2. The crystal field splitting parameter
- The CFSP determines how the octahedron may deviate from a regular octahedron.
- It has a empirical equation, $\Delta_oct = \frac{e|r^4|}{6a^5}q$ 
	- e is the charge on the electrons.
	- q is the charge on the ligands
	- r is the average radius of the d-orbitals.
	- a is the distance between the metal and the ligands.
		- This is almost entirely dependent on the size of the metal ion.
>Empirical equations are not derived mathematically; They are devised from lab experiments.
- Based on this we can generate a list of factors affecting Δ<sub>oct</sub>:

| Factor             | Δ<sub>oct</sub>                |
| ------------------ | ------------------------------ |
| Size of d-orbitals | 3d<4d<5d                       |
| Ionic sizes        | Increases from left-to-right   |
| Oxidation state    | Increases with oxidation state |
