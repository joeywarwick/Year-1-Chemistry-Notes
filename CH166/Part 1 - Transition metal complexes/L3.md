## 2. Spectrochemical series
- From L2, we know that the Δ<sub>oct</sub> is affected by the distance between metal and ligand (M-L).
	- Ligands can affect the Δ<sub>oct</sub>, and consequently the shape too.
- Some ligands lead to a larger Δ<sub>oct</sub> than the others.
> **NOTE** CFT cannot explain this, as it treats all ligands as point charges (See L2). For CFT, chlorides are the same as water.
- If we ranked the Δ<sub>oct</sub> associated with ligands from small to large, we get a series of ligands.
	- Ligands with a **small** Δ<sub>oct</sub>, and hence a small octahedron, are called **weak-field** ligands.
	- Ligands with a **large** Δ<sub>oct</sub>, and hence a large octahedron, are called **strong-field** ligands.
- This series is called the **spectrochemical series**.
	- It is generated by, fittingly, **spectroscopy.**
		- If the energy absorption is **low**, it is **weak-field**.
		- If the energy absorption is **high**, it is **strong-field**.
- A general trend in the series is that strong-field ligands tend to be **dangerous**, because they bind to metals e.g. iron in haemoglobin **irreversibly**.
	- **Strong-field** ligands create stronger M-L bonds, but this isn't fully true.
- Metals and ligands are fairly independent - F<sup>-</sup> is a weaker-field ligand than NH<sub>3</sub>, regardless of the metal.
### 3. Spin-states

	- This leads to nuances when **populating d-orbitals.**
- For d<sup>1-3</sup>, they simply populate the t<sub>2g</sub> orbitals - the metal and ligand have no effect on them.

|               |               | ↑              |
|               |               | ↑              |

- But there are **no more choices for d<sup>8-10</sup>**, as there are **no more spaces in t<sub>2g</sub>.**
> **NOTE** There are **no 'mid-spin' states**, as the pairing energy is either larger than Δ<sub>oct</sub> or not. So all electrons will either adopt high- or low-spin, with no exceptions.
### 3.1. Determining the configuration
- Which configuration the metal adopts is determined by **two factors:**
	- Δ<sub>oct</sub>, which can be broken inro two sub-factors:
		- The metal ion; and
		- The ligand.
	- **Pairing energy**, the energy required to change the spin of a electron and pair it with an opposite-spin electron.
		- **Pairing energy is always the same, regardless of the metal, ligand, or charge.**
> Before splitting, the metal electrons all have the same spin, low-spin requires changing the spin for electron(s). 
-  So if the energy gap is larger than pairing energy, low-spin is preferred.
- We can use this information to predict the electron configuration.

| Metal valence orbital | Spin           |
| --------------------- | -------------- |
| 3d                    | Either         |
| 4d, 5d, etc.          | **Always** low |

| Ion charge | Spin        |
| ---------- | ----------- |
| 2+         | Likely high |
| 3+         | Either      |

| Ligand       | Spin           |
| ------------ | -------------- |
| Strong-field | **Always low** |
| Weak-field   | Likely high    |
### 3.2. Magnetism
- Magnetism arises as a result of electronic configurations.
- There are two main types:
	- **Diamagnetic**, meaning that it is **repelled by magnetic fields.** This requires **all paired electrons.**
	- **Paramagnetic**, meaning that it is **attracted to magnetic fields**. This requires **at least one unpaired electron.**
> **NOTE** This **does not mean that it is a magnet**.
- As the spin state can change electronic configurations, this affects the magnetic properties of the species.
	- Take d<sup>6</sup>
		- **High spin**: t<sub>2g</sub><sup>4</sup> e<sub>g</sub><sup>2</sup>
			- Four unpaired electrons, **paramagnetic**
		- **Low spin** t<sub>2g</sub><sup>6</sup>
			- No unpaired electrons, **diamagnetic.**
