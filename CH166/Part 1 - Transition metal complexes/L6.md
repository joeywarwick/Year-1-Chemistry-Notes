## 2. Ligand Field Theory
- Ligand field theory (LFT) helps to explain why d<sub>0,5,10</sub> orbitals exist despite no stabilisation.
- It is essentially the application of MO theory to TM complexes.
- We recall from MO theory (CH164, L11-15) that orbitals can interact to form σ- or π-bonds.
	- Two types of interactions are relevant to TM complexes:
		- **End-on**, where there is **one point** of overlap, forming a σ-bond; and
		- **Side-on**, where there are **two points** of overlap, forming a π-bond.
- End-on overlap mainly occurs between to form σ-bonds:

| Orbital 1                 | Orbital 2              |
| ------------------------- | ---------------------- |
| s                         | s                      |
| p                         | p                      |
| d<sub>z<sup>2</sup></sub> | s                      |
| d<sub>z<sup>2</sup></sub> | s-p HAO                |
| d<sub>xy, xz, yz</sub>    | p                      |
| d<sub>z<sup>2</sup></sub> | d<sub>xy, xz, yz</sub> |
- Side-on overlap mainly occurs between the following to form π-bonds:

| Orbital 1              | Orbital 2              |
| ---------------------- | ---------------------- |
| p                      | p                      |
| d<sub>xy, xz, yz</sub> | d<sub>xy, xz, yz</sub> |
| d<sub>xy, xz, yz</sub> | p                      |
- There are also symmetry forbidden non-bonding interactions, and they are seen between:

| Orbital 1                 | Orbital 1              |
| ------------------------- | ---------------------- |
| s                         | p                      |
| p                         | p                      |
| d<sub>xy, xz, yz</sub>    | d<sub>xy, xz, yz</sub> |
| d<sub>z<sup>2</sup></sub> | p                      |
- So we already know that constructive overlap leads to stabilisation and a bonding interaction, and vice versa.
	- We also know that the closer the two orbitals are in energy, the larger the degree of overlap and consequently the degree of stabilisation.
- The majority of the electron density will be close to the lower-energy orbital.
### 2.1. LFT for octahedral complexes
- The nine valence orbitals of the metal would be:
	- 1x (n+1)s;
	- 3x (n+1)p; and
	- 5x nd.
		- e.g. Cu: 4s. 4p, and 3d.
- The ligands' orbitals vary between ligands:
	- Hydrides, H<sup>-</sup> -> s;
	- Ammonia, NH<sub>3</sub> -> sp<sup>3</sup>;
	- Halides -> p
- We can evaluate their interactions using SALCs.
	- For metals:
		- s-orbitals are a<sub>1g</sub>;
			- They can combine with six orbitals.
			- They sum up to be equivalent to one bond - each interaction is therefore 1/6 of a bond.
		- p-orbitals are t<sub>1u</sub>;
			- They can combine with two orbitals.
			- So each interaction is 1/2 of one bond.
		- Two d-orbitals are e<sub>g</sub>;
			- d<sub>x<sup>2</sup>-y<sup>2</sup></sub> can interact with four orbitals
				- Each interaction is then 1/4 of a bond.
			- d<sub>z<sup>2</sup></sub> can interact with six orbitals
				- Each interaction is 1/6 of a bond.
		- Three d-orbitals are t<sub>2g</sub>;
			- They are non-bonding, because they cannot form symmetry-permitted interactions.
				- They point between the axis, so all interactions are net-zero interactions.
> a or b means that the orbitals are not degenerate, e means doubly degenerate, and t means triply degenerate.
#### 2.1.1. MOELD
- We can construct a MOELD from the SALCs

| Metal |                | SALCs           |
| ----- | -------------- | --------------- |
| 4p    | Antibonding x3 | t<sub>1u</sub>* |
| 4s    | Antibonding x1 | a<sub>1g</sub>* |
| 3d    | Antibonding x2 | e<sub>g</sub>*  |
| 3d    | Non-bonding x3 |                 |
| 3d    | Bonding x2     | e<sub>g</sub>   |
| 4p    | Bonding x3     | t<sub>1u</sub>  |
| 4s    | Bonding x1     | a<sub>1g</sub>  |

- We can fill this with electrons from both the ligand and the metal ion. 
- Remember that ligands have **lone pairs, so six ligands lead to 12 extra electrons**, in addition to the metal's own electrons.
	- For a d<sup>3</sup>, this means 15 electrons.
#### 2.1.2. Comparing CFT and LFT
- Both result in splitting of d-orbitals to form t<sub>2g</sub> and e<sub>g</sub>.
	- The magnitude of the split in LFT depends on the strength of the bond.
	- So essentially Δ<sub>oct</sub>is dependent on bond strength.
- CFT was unable to explain the existence of Mn<sup>2+</sup>, a d<sup>5</sup> HS. The five electrons would have cancelled out in stabilisation.
	- In LFT, it has 17 electrons (5+12) which are involved in stabilisation.
		- 12 electrons are stabilised as it forms.
- However, if we considered Cr<sup>3+</sup>, a d<sup>3</sup>:
	- The extra stabilisation compared to d<sup>2</sup> is no longer present.
- So the best way to model is to use a hybrid of CFT and LFT.
### 2.2. LFT for non-octahedral complexes
- LFT can be applied to complexes that are non-octahedral too.
#### 2.2.1. LFT for square planar complexes
- Consider square planar complexes.
	- Four ligands at 90 degrees to each other on the xy-plane.
- We consider the metal in the same way. Take a 3rd period transition metal, frontier AOs are:
	- 4s, a<sub>1g</sub>;
		- Very symmetrical
	- 4p, t<sub>1u</sub>;
		- p<sub>x,y</sub> bonding;
		- p<sub>z</sub> cannot form any symmetry-permitted interactions, so non-bonding.
	- 3d, e<sub>g</sub>/t<sub>2g</sub>.
		- The t<sub>2g</sub> orbitals remain non-bonding.
		- e<sub>g</sub> orbitals will interact with the same SALC.
			- The interaction between each individual orbital and the SALC is less.
		- d<sub>x<sup>2</sup>-y<sup>2</sup></sub> is antibonding, so it is higher than z<sup>2</sup>.
- Regardless of CFT or LFT, the d-orbitals are non-degenerate.
