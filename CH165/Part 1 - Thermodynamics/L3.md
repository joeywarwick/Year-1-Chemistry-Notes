## 2. Enthalpy (cont.)
### 2.1. Measuring enthalpy
- We require a zero point to compare the change in enthalpies with.
	- This is the standard enthalpy of formation of the constituent elements in their standard states.
> Standard state is the most stable form at 1 bar and a specific temperature.
#### 2.2.1. Hess's Law
- The total enthalpy change is the sum of all 'mini' enthalpy changes.
- We can formulate this as: $\Delta H_r = \Sigma \Delta H_f (products) - \Sigma \Delta H_f (reactants)$
## 3. Thermodynamic processes
- We can categorise the processes into four categories.
### 3.1. Isothermal
- Temperature remains constant, but heat changes.
### 3.2. Isochoric
- Volume remains constant
- Work = 0. No volume change.
	- dU = dQ.
- Heat capacity is calculated using volume as constant.
	- $c_v = (\frac{dU}{dT})_v$
### 3.3. Isobaric
- Pressure remains constant
### 3.4. Adiabatic
- Adiabatic processes are processes where there is **no heat/mass transfer** between the system and its surroundings.
	- dQ = 0
	- It can be true for isolated systems, or
	- When a process happens instantaneously that only a negligible amount of heat was transferred.
### 3.5. Summary

|               | Isothermal               | Isochoric                 | Isobaric                  | Adiabatic      |
| ------------- | ------------------------ | ------------------------- | ------------------------- | -------------- |
| Constant      | T                        | V                         | p                         | Q              |
| Work          | $W = -\int p \space dV$  | 0                         | $W = -p\Delta V$          | $W = \Delta V$ |
| First Law     | U = W + Q                | U = Q                     | U = W + Q                 | U = W          |
| Heat capacity | Undefined, T is constant | $c_v = (\frac{dU}{dT})_V$ | $c_v = (\frac{dH}{dT})_p$ | 0              |

## 4. Entropy
- Processes tend to have a direction, and they proceed towards a direction spontaneously.
- **Entropy** determines the direction of process.
	- It is a measure of the spatial distribution of energy.
	- This is because '**all spontaneous processes lose heat irrecoverably**'.
### 4.1. Quantification of entropy
- Entropy is again a **state function**.
- The change in entropy is defined as a function of the temperature and change in internal energies.
	- $dS = \frac{dQ_{rev}}{T}$
	- $\Delta S = \int \frac{dQ_{rev}}{T}$
		- For the same amount of work/heat:
			- At higher temperatures the change in entropy is proportionally lower
		- For the same temperature:
			- At higher work done/heat the change in entropy is proportionally higher
>**NOTE** Reversible changes' entropy change is always 0.
### 4.2. Second law of thermodynamics
- It is important to consider the surroundings too.
	- $\Delta S_{univ} = \Delta S_{surrounding} + \Delta S_{system}$
- For a reaction to be spontaneous, 
	- Δ<sub>univ</sub> > 0.
### 4.3. Third law of thermodynamics
- The entropy change of any process tends towards 0 as the temperature approaches 0 K.
> A pure and perfect crystalline substance at 0K will have **zero entropy**.
- So we can find the absolute entropy.
> **NOTE** Recall that we cannot find absolute enthalpy.
## 5. Gibbs Free Energy
- AKA Gibbs energy, free energy, GFE
- Gibbs Free Energy, ΔG, measures the spontaneity of a reaction.
	- ΔG < 0, the process is spontaneous
	- ΔG > 0, the process is unfeasible.
- ΔG stems from the second law and entropy:
	- As the amount of entropy lost by the system is the amount of entropy gained by the surroundings, and
	- $dS = \frac{dQ}{T} = \frac{dH}{T}$
		- $dS_{surr} = -\frac{dH_{sys}}{T}$
		- $dS_{uni} = -\frac{dH_{sys}}{T} + dS_{sys}$
		- $-T \Delta S_{uni} = \Delta H_{sys} - T \Delta S_{sys}$
			- We here define -TΔS<sub>uni</sub> as ΔG.
		- $\Delta G = \Delta H - T \Delta S$
		- Integrating yields G = H - TS.
### 5.1. Helmholtz Free Energy
- Same as Gibbs:
	- $\Delta A = \Delta U - T \Delta S$
	- This is for constant volume.
> As no work can be done the enthalpy change is replaced with change in heat.
- Whichever state has lower Gibbs will be the more favourable state.
> **NOTE** GFE is **not** conserved like energy.
### 5.2. Endergonic and exergonic
> **NOTE** **Not** the same as exo/endothermic
- Spontaneous processes are called **exergonic**.
- Non-spontaneous processes are called **endergonic**.
