## 2. Critical points (continued)
- The boiling curve stops at the critical point (See L4).
	- It has a positive gradient generally
- Above the critical point, there is no phase transition.
- We can formulate the lines that connect the critical point on other planes.
	- Our current curve is on the pressure/temperature plane.
- Assuming constant temperature (isotherms), we can formulate curves on the pressure/volume plane.
	- Below the critical temperature:
		- $pV = nRT, V \approx \frac{1}{p}$
			- pV is a constant.
		- So an exponential decay curve decays until we hit the p<sub>vap</sub>.
			- Here gas transitions to liquid.
		- Then it basically suddenly drops and plateaus.
			- Little to no volume decrease with further pressure.
	- Above the critical pressure:
		- No phase transition, so smooth decay curve.
## 3. Phase diagram
- We have already discussed the boiling curve.
- The line between solid and liquid is melting line.
- The line between solid and gas is sublimation line.
- There is another important point: the triple point.
	- The triple point is where all three phases co-exist in equilibrium.
		- The chemical potentials are equal.
### 3.1. The melting line
- The Clapeyron equation modelled the gradient of the vaporisation line.
	- The melting line can be modelled similarly.
- When something melts/vaporises/sublimes:
	- The enthalpy of melting is positive.
	- The entropy change is also positive.
	- The volume change is positive.
- The gradient of the melting line is positive.
- The same p/T equilibration process for boiling holds for melting too.
	-  More solids melt at higher temp, which equilibrates.
	- More solids melt at lower pressure, which equilibrates.
#### 3.1.1. Water
- For water, the density of the solid is less than the liquid.
	- Volume change is negative.
	- So $\frac{\Delta_{trs}H}{T\Delta_{trs}V} < 0$
- So melting line's gradient is negative.
## 4. Equilibrium
- Most reactions are reversible.
- When equilibrium is reached:
	- The forward and backwards reaction occurs at the same rate.
	- Overall there is no net change in concentrations.
### 4.1. Factors influencing on temperature
- For exothermic reactions such as ammonia formation:
- $N_2 + 3H_2 \rightleftharpoons 2NH_3$ 
	- Lower pressure favours forward reaction.
	- Lower pressure favours forward reaction (less mols).
- vice versa.
- Catalysts lower E<sub>a</sub>, and speeds up reactions equally both ways.
	- So no effect on equilibrium position.
### 4.2. Gibbs energy and equilibrium
- At constant temperature and pressure:
	- The reaction rates will adapt such that the Gibbs energy is minimised.
	- This changes the composition of the reaction mixture.
#### 4.2.1. Extent of reactions
- If we define ζ (zeta) as the extent of the reaction, where:
	- If all reactants were converted to products, ζ = 1
	- If no reactants were converted to products, ζ = 0.
- So we can write this for Gibbs:
	- At constant T and p, $dG =\mu_Adn_{A} + \mu_Adn_A$
		- $dn_B = d\zeta$
		- $dn_A = -d\zeta$
	- $dG= -\mu_Ad\zeta + \mu_Bd\zeta$
	- $(\frac{\partial G}{\partial \zeta})_{p, T} = \mu_B - \mu_A = \Delta_rG$
- But minimum of G is dependent on T. 
	- We use the standard pressure for this.
#### 4.2.2. Pressure and Gibbs
- Looking at our fundamental equation:
	- If temperature and composition is constant (at equilibrium), then it boils down to $dG = Vdp$
	- We can integrate both sides and substitute in the ideal gas equation.
		- $\Delta G = \int^{G_2}_{G_1} dG = \int^{p_2}_{p_1}V dp = \int^{p_2}_{p_1}V \frac{nRT}{p} dp = nRT \ln{\frac{p_2}{p_1}}$ 
	- Remember that the Gibbs in compared to standard pressure!
		- $G_1 = G^O$
>**NOTE** Here the pressure is in bars. 1 bar = 100 kPa.
#### 4.2.3. Equilibrium constant
- Utilising the equation derived:
	- $\Delta G =  nRT \ln{\frac{p_2}{p_1}}$
- We define  p<sub>2</sub>/p<sub>1</sub> as Q, the reaction quotient. 
	- It is the ratio of the partial pressures of the reactant and product.
- We know that at equilibrium that ΔG = 0
	- $\Delta_rG^O = -RT \ln{Q}$
> **NOTE** The reaction quotient at equilibrium is the **equilibrium constant**, K.
- So we can write:
	- $\Delta_rG^O = -RT \ln{K}$
### 4.3. Equilibrium of general gas phase reactions
- For a general reaction we can write:
	- $\nu_AA+\nu_BB \rightarrow \nu_CC+\nu_DD$
- We also need to consider stoichiometry. $dn_A = -\nu d\zeta$
- Then assuming constant temperature and pressure, we can write:
	- $dG = -\mu_A\nu_Ad\zeta -\mu_B\nu_Bd\zeta + \mu_C\nu_Cd\zeta + \mu_D\nu_Dd\zeta$ 
- Collecting this:
	- $\Delta_rG = \sum{\nu_j\mu_j} + RT \sum{\nu_j \ln{\frac{p_j}{p}}}$
- The K<sub>p</sub> can be therefore worked out from another equation I can't be asked to type out.
	- From the above we reach:
		- $\Delta_rG^O = -RT \ln{K}$
#### 4.3.1. Le Chatelier principle
- The principle states that 'when a change is applied the equilibrium shifts to partially counteract this change'.
> **NOTE** The equilibrium constant, K, is **independent** of the pressure, but the composition is not.
#### 4.3.2. van't Hoff equation
- The van't Hoff equation models how the equilibrium constant, K, changes with temperature.
