## 2. Gibbs energy (cont.)

### 2.1. Chemical reactions
- The reaction's  ΔG =  ΔG<sub>products</sub> -  ΔG<sub>reactants</sub>
#### 2.1.2. Gibbs for pK<sub>a</sub>
- - Gibbs can also be used to predict pK<sub>a</sub>, the acid dissociation constant.
	- The more positive the Gibbs is, the less dissociation, hence higher pK<sub>a</sub>.
### 2.2. Natural variables
- Natural variables are convenient variables used to describe state functions.
- The most important one is the one regarding Gibbs, temperature, and pressure.
	- $\Delta G = V\Delta p - S\Delta T$
		- This equation comes from $G = H - TS$
		- $dG = dH - d(TS)$
			- $H = U + pV$
			- $dH = dU + pdV + Vdp$
		- $dG = dU + pdV + Vdp - TdS - SdT$
			- $dU = dQ + dW$
				- $TdS = dQ$
				- $-pdV = W$
			- $dU = TdS - pdV$
## 3. Phase transitions
- Phases are forms of matter that is uniform both chemically and physically.

### 3.1. Chemical potential
- Gibbs in extensive, it depends on the quantity and state..
	- It is somewhat in equilibrium thought.
- So it's insufficient to say that the Gibbs of one state has to be lower.
	- The Gibbs changes depending on the quantity of material present in each phase.
- An extra state function required.
- In addition to the basic $dG = Vdp - SdT$ , to address how Gibbs changes with the amount of matter in each phase.
	- $dG = Vdp - SdT + \mu_{gas}dn_{gas} + \mu_{liq}dn_{liq}$
- μ is the **chemical potential**. 
	- It is an intrinsic property
	- It is essentially $(\frac{\partial G}{\partial n_i})_{T, p}$ 
> **NOTE** In equilibrium, the chemical potential is the same across phases.
 - This is because dn<sub>gas</sub> = -dn<sub>liq</sub>, then 0 = μ<sub>gas</sub>dn<sub>gas</sub> - μ<sub>liq</sub>dn<sub>gas</sub>.
> **NOTE** We can view chemical potential as molar Gibbs energy under constant T and p.
### 3.2. Vaporisation
- Trivially:
	- $\Delta_{vap}H = H_{gas} - H_{liq}$
	- $\Delta_{vap}S = S_{gas} - S_{liq}$
- We know that vaporisation is always endothermic, so for it to be spontaneous, the entropy change must also be positive.
- At equilibrium:
	- ΔH = TΔS
- Increasing temperature:
	- ΔH < TΔS
	- ΔG < 0
	- Vaporisation favoured
- Decreasing temperature:
	- ΔH > TΔS
	- ΔG > 0. 
	- Condensation favoured
#### 3.2.1. Boiling point
- The temperature at which the liquid and gas phases are in equilibrium is the **boiling point** (T<sub>b</sub>).
	- Their molar Gibbs energies are equal.
		- μ<sub>liq</sub> = μ<sub>gas</sub>
	- $\Delta_{vap}G = G_{gas} - G_{liq} = 0$
		- $\Delta_{vap}G = \Delta_{vap}H - T_b\Delta_{vap}S = 0$
	- $T_b = \frac{\Delta_{vap}H}{\Delta_{vap}S}$
#### 3.2.2. Trouton's Rule
- Rearranging $\Delta_{vap}G = \Delta_{vap}H - T_b\Delta_{vap}S = 0$ gives $\Delta_{vap}H = T_b\Delta_{vap}S$
	- This is a linear graph in y = mx+c form.
- So plotting a graph of vaporisation enthalpy against vaporisation entropy gives a **straight line**.
	- The standard entropy of vaporisation is 87-89 J K<sup>-1</sup> mol<sup>-1</sup> for many liquids. 
		- We can use this to estimate standard enthalpy of vaporisation.
			- $\Delta H \approx T_b \times 85$
- The Trouton rule has exceptions:
	- Particle interaction in gas phase:
		- HF, 
			- Hexamer rings
		- HCOOH
			- H-bonds
		- Entropy change will be only ~60
	- Particle interaction is liquid phase:
		- Water
			- H-bonds
		- Entropy change ~110
#### 3.2.3. Boiling curve
- We start from a system where everything is in gas phase.
- Pressure change:
	- As pressure increases, as some point liquid will begin to form.
		- This pressure value is the **equilibrium vapour pressure**.
	- If the pressure further increases:
		- More gas condensates;
		- So less gas;
		- Pressure equilibrates.
			- **The pressure inside remains the equilibrium pressure.**
	- If all of the gas condensates:
		- Only liquid, very difficult to compress.
- Temperature change:
	- At temperatures above T<sub>b</sub>:
		- The system is entirely gaseous
	- As temperature decreases:
		- More liquid condensates
		- Releasing energy
		- Equilibrate temperature
			- **The temperature of the system is still T<sub>b</sub>.
	- If temperature further decreases:
		- System is wholly liquid
		- Temperature can go below T<sub>b</sub>.
> The energy consumed in phase change is the **latent heat**.
#### 3.2.4. Evaporation below T<sub>b</sub>
- Liquids evaporate event at ambient pressures.
- Water evaporates because the air is not at vapor pressure equilibrium with the liquid water.
> The equilibrium pressure is usually known as '100% humidity'.
#### 3.2.5. Clapeyron equation
- The Clapeyron equation tells us about the gradient of the co-existence line (The line which equilibrium points sit on).
- $\frac{dP}{dT} = \frac{\Delta_{trs}S}{\Delta_{trs}V} = \frac{\Delta_{trs}H}{T\Delta_{trs}V}$
- This is the general Clapeyron equation, but as we can estimate volume change for liquid-gas transition, we can take this a step further.
#### 3.2.6. Clausius-Clapeyron equation
- As the volume of the gas would be much larger than volume of liquid, we can estimate **volume change to be the volume of the gas.**
	- From pV = nRT, $V = \frac{RT}{p}$
- We also assume that ΔH is independent of the temperature.

## 4. Critical point
- The p/T **boiling** curve does not extend infinitely. It stops at the **critical point**.
- Near the critical point:
	- Properties change greatly:
		- Liquids become compressible
		- Deviation from ideal gases
- At the critical point:
	- Only one phase exists
	- Δ<sub>vap</sub>H = 0
- Above critical point:
	- Lower viscosity than liquid
	- Can dissolve materials like liquid
	- No surface tension
