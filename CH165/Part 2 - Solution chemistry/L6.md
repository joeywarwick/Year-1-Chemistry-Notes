## 2. Electrolyte solutions
- Electrolytes are ions which form from the dissociation of salt in a solvent.
	- We call the solvent the **major** component; and
	- we call the solute the **minor** component.
### 2.1. Water
- Consider water:
	- O-H bond length 0.957 angstroms
		- Polarised with dipole moments towards oxygen.
		- **Charge asymmetry** from a large dipole moment.
			- The dipole moment is 1.84 D (Debyes), which is equal to C m (Coulomb metres).
	- Bond angle 104.5 degrees
		- 2 Lone pairs
#### 2.1.1. Flickering cluster model
- Water forms hydrogen bonds between each other.
	- So they form instantaneous clusters of water molecules held together in a hydrogen-bonding network.
	- 80% of water is in the network at any one point, and 20% is monomeric.
	- They only exist for ~1 picosecond in each position.
		- This can be determined with X-ray diffraction.
- When water is cooled down sufficiently, it forms **ice-like** regions. 
	- These regions have 100% **tetrahedral** arrangement of water.
	- This holds water molecules apart
		- So less density.
#### 2.1.2. Other group 6 hydrides
- Other group 6 hydrides rely on (induced) dipole-dipole interactions.
	- So less energy required to boil and melt.
- Hydrogen has hydrogen bonds
	- So anomalous compared to other hydrides.
### 2.2.Thermodynamics
- An energy cycle can be used to calculate the Δ<sub>solv</sub>H.
	- $AB \rightarrow A^+ + B^-$
		- Lattice enthalpy, a lot of energy required
	- $A^+_{(g)} + B^-_{(g)}\rightarrow A^+_{(aq)} + B^-_{(aq)}$
		- Δ<sub>hyd</sub>H, very negative
	- We can use these two to calculate solvation enthalpies.
#### 2.2.1. Bulk structure of water
- Δ<sub>hyd</sub>H is very negative.
	- When ions are added to water, it forms **solvation shells.**
	- It disrupts the existing structure and forces water to arrange itself around it.
		- This is the **primary** solvation shell. This is where the water acts like a ligand.
	- The water reforms around the primary solvation shell to form the **secondary** solvation shell.
	- The next part out is disordered. It's simply called the **disordered region**.
	- This is very favourable.
- This model only applies when there is sufficient space between ions such that there is enough space for water molecule to surround the ion.
	- As the concentration decreases the separation increases.
- So this model only applies under **dilute** conditions.
	- Usually <0.001 M.
#### 2.2.2. Types of ions
- Ions are classified as **structure-making** or **structure-breaking** depending on their ability to order water.
	- Ions with a high charge density, like:
		- Li
		- F
		- Mg
		- Al
		- They are **structure-making** and have **large** solvation shells.
			- Here the two solvation shells dominate over the disordered area.
	- Ions with low charge density, like:
		- Rb
		- Cs
		- I
		- Br
		- They are **structure-breaking** and have smaller solvation shells.
			- So the disordered region dominates over solvation shells.
> **NOTE** Regardless of whether an ion is SM or SB, Δ<sub>hyd</sub>H is always energetically favourable (negative).
- Δ<sub>hyd</sub>H is more negative for SM ions. 
	- ΔS is more positive for SM ions.
#### 2.2.3. Partial molar volume
- We know that molar volume $V_m = \frac{V}{m}$
- When two quantities of the same pure liquid are mixed together, it forms a volume equal to the two added together.
- But this is not the case for mixtures of liquids.
	- The interactions are different.
	- We can define the volume with regards to the moles of each liquid present in the mixture.
	- $V_m = (\frac{\partial V}{\partial n_i})_{p,T,n_j}$
		- When we add 1 mol of water to water, the volume increases by 18 cm<sup>3</sup>. This is the molar volume of water in water.
		- When we add 1 mol of water to a lot of ethanol, the volume only increases by 13.8 cm<sup>3</sup>.
	- We can see a general trend where the volume that water occupies decreases with increasing ethanol mole fraction.
- For salts, we can assess their effects on a graph of solution volume against number of moles of salt.
	- If the gradient is positive, then V<sub>m</sub> > 0.
		- Because the solution is expanding.
	- If the gradient is negative, then V<sub>m</sub> < 0.
		- Because the solution is contracting. 
- A SM ion will pull water towards itself.
	- So V<sub>m</sub> < 0.
- A SB ion will create more disorder, so solution expands.
	- Then V<sub>m</sub> > 0.
> This provides a more definitive boundary between SM and SB ions.
#### 2.2.4. Ideal solution behaviour
> **NOTE** Different from ideal **gas** behaviour.
- Chemical potential, $\mu_A = (\frac{\partial G}{\partial nA})$
	- G is Gibbs
	- A is number of moles in the mixture. Consider a mixture of two liquids A and B.
	- Both liquids are volatile and are capable of going into the gas phase.
		- There is a equilibrium of gas phase and liquid phase molecules for both components.
		- The pressure $\approx$ concentration of A and B in gas phase.
- The μ is dependent of the solution composition, and can be modelled by the following equation:
	-  $\mu_A = \mu_A* + RT \ln{\frac{p_A}{p_A*}}$
		- μ<sub>A</sub>* is the μ for **pure** A
		- p<sub>A</sub> is the p for **pure** A.
#### 2.2.5. Raoult's law
- For an **ideal solution**, $p_A = p_A^* \times x_A$
	- x<sub>A</sub> is the mole fraction of A in the solution.
	- Because the more A there is, the more A in the gas phase!
- We can substitute this into the above equation, to give:
	- $\mu_A = \mu_A* + RT \ln{x_A}$
- Raoult's law is only obeyed when:
	- A-A, B-B, and A-B interactions are very similar.
		- Water and deuterated water
		- Aromatic molecules
	- When the solutions are so dilute that A is more likely to be surround by other A.
#### 2.2.6. Energetics of liquid-liquid mixing
- Again, $\Delta G_{mix} = \Delta H_{mix} - T \Delta S_{mix}$
	- For an ideal solution, ΔH<sub>mix</sub> = 0.
	- Where components mix, ΔH < 0
		- This makes ΔS positive, so will mix.
	- Where components do not mix, ΔH > 0.
		- AKA **immiscible behaviour**.
#### 2.2.7. Deviation from ideal solution behaviour
- Raoult's law states that x<sub>A</sub> and p<sub>A</sub> should be a linear relationship.
	- This is only true for ideal solutions.
- Where A-B interaction is weaker, more A enters gas phase.
	- So positive deviation from Raoult's.
	- μ is **greater** than μ in ideal solution.
		- So the solution is less stable, as systems tend to exist in lowest energy state.
> μ is gradient of Gibbs. 
- Where A-B interaction is stronger, less A enters gas phase, as it is held more tightly.
	- So negative deviation from Raoults.
	- μ is smaller than μ in ideal solution.
