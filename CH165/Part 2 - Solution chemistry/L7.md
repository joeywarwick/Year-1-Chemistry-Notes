## 2. Ideal solution behaviour (cont.)
### 2.1. Henry's Law
- A linear line can be drawn for the gradient of the deviation at lower mole fractions.
	- The gradient of this line is the **Henry's constant**, K<sub>H</sub>.
		- This holds for dilute solutions.
	- The line describes the relationship between the solubility and the partial pressure above the liquid.
- The lower the K<sub>H</sub>, the higher the solubility.
	- Less liquid in gas phase.
### 2.2. Modelling deviation
- For intermediate regions where neither Raoult's and Henry's applies, we will use a measure known as **activities**.
	- $a_A = \frac{p_A}{p_A^*} =\gamma_Ax_A$
		- γ is the activity coefficient.
			- We can calculate this, which tells us the extent of deviation from ideal solution behaviour.
	- From the original $\mu_A = \mu_A* + RT \ln{\frac{p_A}{p_A*}}$
		- We can substitute and give $\mu_A = \mu_A* + RT \ln{x_A} + RT \ln{\gamma_A}$
- Where γ:
	- **= 1**, then ideal solution behaviour.
	- **> 1**, then positive deviation.
	- **< 1**, then negative deviation.
- $a_A = c_A\gamma_A$
	- a is activity;
	- c is concentration;
	- γ is activity coefficient.
> **NOTE** The concentration should be normalised, yielding no units.
### 2.3. General characteristics of ideal solutions
1. Obeys Raoult's.
	- $p_A = p_A^*x_A$
2. No heat change with mixing
	- $\Delta H_{mix} = 0$
3. No total volume change when mixing
	- No expansion or contraction
4. Random mixing for molecules.
	- No preference for AA, BB, or AB interactions.
### 2.4. Idealness of electrolyte solutions
- Consider a electrolyte solution that comprises of water...
	- Solvent
	- Volatile
	- Major phase
- ...and a salt:
	- Solute
	- Non-volatile
	- Minor phase
- Water does not follow Raoult's in this case.
	- There is a mixing enthalpy.
> **NOTE** Activities must be used here.
- The solution also does not obey Raoult's
	- There is a preference for certain interaction over others.
	- There is contraction/expansion.
> Electrolyte solutions are stabilising at lower concentrations.
#### 2.4.1. Electroneutrality
- When ions are dissolved in water, they do not simply exist as a charged ion.
	- According to Coulomb's law, it will attract the opposite charge to surround itself.
	- This forms a 'cloud' of negative charges known as the **ion atmosphere**.
		- This balances out the negative charge.
- This prevents the random arrangement of ion and water in solution.
> **NOTE** Both the ion cloud and hydration shells are present on an ion.
#### 2.4.2. Colligative properties
- How the the vapour pressure of a **non-volatile** solute changes does not depend on the identity of the solute.
	- This is the **colligative property**.
#### 2.4.3. Activity coefficients of electrolytes
- At the low-concentration region, the γ of electrolytes drop from 1.
	- They are more energetically stable than ideal solutions.
- They then increase and can go **above 1**.
	- So now due to the number of ions the solution has actually been destabilised.
#### 2.4.4. Debye-Huckel theory
- We can model the low concentration region to find γ.
	- This is the Debye-Huckel theory, which considers:
		- Ion-solvent interactions;
		- Modified solvent-solvent;
		- Electrostatics.
			- D-H assumes that the only electrostatic interactions occur between ions-ions.
- The equation is as the following:
	- $\log{\gamma \pm} = -A|z_+z_-|\sqrt{I}$
		- A is a constant, 0.509
		- z<sub>+,-</sub> are the charges on the cations and anions.
		- I is ionic strength.
- We will need to calculate ionic strength:
	- $I = \frac{1}{2}\sum{z_i^2m_i}$
		- z is again charge
		- m is molarity, or concentration.
> We can see that γ is high dependent on the charge of the ions.
- The electrolytes only obey D-H up to a certain point (only at lower conc.)
>**NOTE** The original Debye-Huckel theory used molality.
>	- Molality is concentration/mass, molarity is concentration/volume.
>		- Mass is independent of T and p
>			- Same for mole fractions
>		- Volume is not independent of T and p.
>	- At lower concentrations:
>		- Molarity = Molality
>		- Concentration = Activity
#### 2.4.5. Applications of Debye-Huckel
- D-H is used in both Kinetics (see Part 3) and thermodynamics.
	- By adding large quantities of a salt that does not take part in the reaction ('inert salt');
	- A stable ion atmosphere (see above) forms, stabilising the ions.
	- So equilibrium shifts to the right.
## 3. Intro to acid-base chemistry
### 3.1. pH
- $pH = -\log{[H_3O^+}]$
### 3.2. Equilibrium
- There have been various definitions of acids:
	- Arrhenius: 'Acids are compounds that form protons in aqueous solutions. Bases form OH<sup>-</sup> ions.'
	- Bronsted-Lowry; 'Acids donate protons, Bases accept protons.'
- In the following reaction:
	- $HCl + H_2O \rightleftharpoons Cl^- + H_3O^+$
		- HCl is acid, because it donates protons
		- H<sub>2</sub>O is base, because it accepts protons.
- We can see that every acid has a conjugate base (HCl and Cl<sup>-</sup>), and every base has a conjugate acid (H<sub>2</sub>O and H<sub>3</sub>O<sup>+</sup>).
#### 3.2.1. Amphotericity
- If a molecule can act as both an acid and a base it is **amphoteric**.
### 3.3. Strength of acids
- We can measure the strength of acids by their tendency to donate protons.
	- More protons donated means more dissociation means stronger acid.
	- Less protons donated means less dissociation means weaker acid.
- We quantify this with the **acid dissociation constant**, or K<sub>a</sub>.
	- We have already defined K<sub>c</sub> for the general process of an acid in water:
		- $K_c = \frac{[A^-][H_3O^+]}{[HA][H_2O]}$
		- $K_a = \frac{[A^-][H_3O^+]}{[HA]}$
> **NOTE** This is because in a dilute solution concentration of water is basically constant.
- Higher the K<sub>a</sub>, the stronger the acid, as more dissociation occurs.
	- If **K<sub>a</sub> > 1**, then more acid dissociates than not.
- $pK_a = -\log{K_a}$
	- Stronger the acid, the more negative the pK<sub>a</sub>.
