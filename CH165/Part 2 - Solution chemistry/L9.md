## 2. Solution conductivity
### 2.1. Molar conductivity
- Conductivity is conductance with area factored in.
	- Molar conductivity, **Λ** (lambda) is conductivity with concentration factored in.
- Only charged species can conduct.
	- We ignore neutral species.
- So depending on the strength of an electrolyte, it's charge-carrying capabilities will be different.
	- Strong electrolytes will have a great extent of dissociation, e.g. strong acids.
		- We assume that it dissociates near completely.
	- Weak electrolytes only dissociate partially.
		- The undissociated species do not contribute to conductivity.
- Consider a strong electrolyte.
	- Conductivity is proportional to the concentration, so we write:
		- $\Lambda_m = \frac{K}{c}, in \space \Omega^{-1} \space mol^{-1} \space cm^2$
- In the ideal case, Λ<sub>m</sub> is a constant. 
#### 2.1.1. Molar concentration at infinite dilution
- Λ<sub>0</sub> is the molar concentration at infinite dilution.
	- This is where Λ obeys the ideal case.
- In standard cases:
	- Strong electrolytes deviate negatively from the ideal case slightly
	- Weak electrolytes deviate negatively from the ideal case greatly.
> The shape of these curves reflect K<sub>a</sub>.
- On this basis, the molar conductivity of weak and strong electrolytes both decrease with increasing concentration. 
	- Strong electrolytes decrease slightly
	- Weak electrolytes decrease greatly.
- At infinite dilution:
	- $\Lambda_0 = \Lambda_{strong} = \Lambda_{weak}$
> Infinite dilution means that the ions are effectively infinitely separated and do not interact. 
#### 2.1.2. Factors affecting molar conductivity
- There are three main reason to the deviation of **strong electrolytes**:
	1. Retardation effect
		- The ions carry with it a oppositely-charged ion atmosphere which is repelled by the electrodes.
		- As it approaches the electrode it accelerates towards the electrode while the ion atmosphere drags it back, slowing it down.
		- This is the **retardation effect**.
	2. Electrophoretic effect
		- The ions carry a hydration shell in addition to the ion atmosphere.
		- It therefore carries a large 'blob' of particles
		- So it is hard to push through water molecules carrying these particles.
		- So slowed down.
		- Effect intensifies with amount of ion in solution.
	3. Ion pairing (high conc.)
		- At higher concentrations ions can be forced to come together.
		- Take Cd<sup>2+</sup> and F<sup>-</sup>, when they come together they form CdF<sup>+</sup>.
		- They now have weaker net charge
		- So force of attraction is weaker.
#### 2.1.3. Molar conductivity for weak electrolytes
- For weak electrolytes, only a small fraction dissociates. So we need to consider degree of dissociation.
> Neutral (undissociated) species do not conduct.
- We can model the degree of ionisation using the following equation:
	- $\alpha = \frac{\Lambda_m}{\Lambda_o}$
	- This is molar conductivity divided by molar conductivity at infinite dilution.
- Consider weak acid A:
	- If the nominal (assume no dissociation) concentration of HA is c, we can write:
		- $[H_3O^+] = \alpha c$
		- $[A^-] = \alpha c$
		- $[HA] = (1-\alpha) c$
- Substituting this into expression of K<sub>a</sub> yields expression I am too lazy to write in LaTeX.
	- You can further substitute in $\alpha = \frac{\Lambda_m}{\Lambda_o}$ to yield another expression I am too lazy to write.
#### 2.1.4. Kohlrausch's law
- At low concentrations, **strong electrolytes** obey the following:
	- $\Lambda_m = \Lambda_0 - K\sqrt{c}$
		- This is effectively a y=mx+c-styled graph.
		- Λ<sub>0</sub> is the intercept, and K (conductivity) is the gradient.
	- Weak electrolytes' partial dissociation equilibrium prevent this from being valid.
#### 2.1.5. Kohlrausch's law of independent migration
- We can use this to determine the Λ<sub>0</sub> for weak electrolytes.
- At infinite dilution:
	- $\Lambda_0 = n\Lambda_0^+ + n\Lambda_0^-$
	- As it is infinite dilution, we assume that:
		- The ions do not interact.
		- The ions are infinitely separated.
- For example, to determine Λ<sub>0</sub> for NaCl:
	- $\Lambda_{0_{NaCl}} = \Lambda_{0_{Na^+}} + \Lambda_{0_{Cl^-}}$
- But for AlCl<sub>3</sub>:
	- $\Lambda_{0_{AlCl_3}} = \Lambda_{0_{Na^+}} + 3\Lambda_{0_{Cl^-}}$
- To find Λ<sub>0</sub> for CH<sub>3</sub>COOH, we can simply consider Λ<sub>0</sub> for strong electrolytes:
	- $\Lambda_{0_{CH_3COOH}} = \Lambda_{0_{CH_3COONa}} + \Lambda_{0_{HCl}} - \Lambda_{0_{NaCl}}$
	- Consider the Λ<sub>0</sub> added and the Λ<sub>0</sub> subtracted:

| Added                         | Subtracted     | Remaining                     |
| ----------------------------- | -------------- | ----------------------------- |
| CH<sub>3</sub>COO<sup>-</sup> |                | CH<sub>3</sub>COO<sup>-</sup> |
| Na<sup>+</sup>                | Na<sup>+</sup> |                               |
| Cl<sup>-</sup>                | Cl<sup>-</sup> |                               |
| H<sup>+</sup>                 |                | H<sup>+</sup>                 |
- Because $\Lambda_0 = n\Lambda_0^+ + n\Lambda_0^-$ this will give the Λ<sub>0</sub> for CH<sub>3</sub>COOH.
> In general, three strong electrolytes which can give the weak electrolyte via addition/subtraction should be found.
### 2.2. Speed of ions
- We knew that conductivity is dependent on the concentration, the speed, and the charge of ions.
	- At infinite dilution we have removed the concentration factor.
- So Λ<sub>0</sub> is essentially the speed of the ion.
	- But we see varying trends for Λ<sub>0</sub> in regards to the size of the ion.
		- Group 1 metal ions increase in speed with size
		- Nitrogen ions (NH<sub>4</sub>, NMe<sub>4</sub>, etc.) decrease in speed with size.
	- Why might this be the case?
#### 2.2.1. Stokes law model
- Consider a singly-charged positive ion placed between two electrodes.
	- When the electrodes are switched on it experiences a force of attraction.
		- The strength of the attraction, $F = zeE$
			- z is the charge of the ion
			- e is the electron charge
			- E is electric field strength.
				- $E = \frac{V}{l}$
	- It begins to accelerate towards towards the cathode.
		- But it is slowed down by the frictional force.
			- We call this force the **retarding force**, F*.
			- $F* = fs = 6\pi\eta as$
				- f is coefficient of friction;
				- s is speed of ion;
				- η is the viscosity of the solution.
	- It reaches a drift velocity and keeps that velocity.
- Combining all we can say that:
	- $s = \frac{zeE}{ 6\pi\eta a}$
	- Note that a includes both the radius of the ion and the hydration shell.
- The reason that group 1 ions show increasing speed is because theyu become less structure-breaking down the group (See L6). 
	- Li<sup>+</sup> is structure-making, so will carry a lot of water. 
		- So a will be very high
			- So lower speed.
	- K<sup>+</sup> is structure breaking, so will carry few water.
		- So a will be quite small
			- So higher speed.
- Conversely, we can say that the nitrogen ions are not very solvated and have effective no solvation shells.
	- They follow size trends.
#### 2.2.2. Proton
- Protons move very very quickly.
	- Protons do not have to move between water. It can protonate water like a chain:
		- $(H_3O^+)_1 + (H_2O)_2 \rightarrow (H_2O)_1 + (H_3O^+)_2$
	- It 'jumps' between water molecules.
		- This is called the **Grotthus mechanism**.
- The rate is, however, limited by the **alignment** of water molecules (the rate limiting step)
	- Water molecules have to be in the correct alignment to allow protons to 'jump'.
> Proton movement is sped up in ice, because everything is locked in the correct position.
## 3. Intro to redox chemistry
- Redox reactions are reactions where oxidations and reductions both occur.
	- OILRIG
> **NOTE** A reduction does no occur without oxidation, and vice versa. They all occur at some point in the system.
### 3.1. Oxidising and reducing agents
- Oxidising agents are reduced as they increase the oxidation state of something else
- Reducing agents are oxidised as the decrease the oxidation state of something else.
