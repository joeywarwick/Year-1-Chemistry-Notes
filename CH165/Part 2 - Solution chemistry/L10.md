### 2.1. Half-cell equations
- We can break a redox equation down to two half-cell equations.
	- They describe the electron transfer between species.
- For example, for $2HCl + 2Na \rightarrow 2NaCl + H_2$
	- $2Na \rightarrow 2Na^+ + 2e^-$
	- $2H^+ + 2e^- \rightarrow H_2$
### 2.2. Spontaneity of redox reactions
- We can measure how easily redox reactions occur with Gibbs.
	- The equilibrium constant, K, can tell us whether the metal wants to exist as ions or as metal.
- Consider Cu<sup>2+</sup> and Zn. 
	- $Cu^{2+} + Zn \rightarrow Zn^{2+} + Cu$
	- We visually know that Zn would be oxidised, but what if we cannot visually check?
- We use electrode potentials in conjunction with $\Delta G = -RT \ln{K}$ (See L5).
### 2.3. Electrochemical Potentials
- Consider a general equation for a redox reaction:
	- $A + ne^- \rightarrow B$
	- By placing electrodes with electrolytes, a electric potential (voltage) is generated.
- For a metal electrode-metal electrolyte system:
	- Cu<sup>2+</sup> | Cu
	- $Cu^{2+} + 2e^- \rightleftharpoons Cu$
- Another common situation is where the electrode is **inert** and only provides electrons:
	- Pt, Au, graphite...
#### 2.3.1. Measuring electrochemical potentials
- We can measure the electrochemical potential (in V) between a electrode and electrolyte.
	- We connect one end of a digital voltmeter (DVM) to the electrode to be measured and immerse it in the electrolyte solutions.
	- We connect the other end to a terminal with a well-known electrode/electrolyte half-cell with a known electrochemical potential.
		- The positive terminal is connected to the cathode (reduction).
		- The negative is connected to the anode (oxidation).
>**NOTE** The DVM has a very high internal resistance. The prevents the half-cells being drained beyond the initial establishment of equilibrium, as no current is constantly passed through.:
- A salt bridge is used to separate the two half-cells, so you do not have to mix them together.
#### 2.3.2. Calculating electrochemical potentials
- Consider the same reaction between Zinc and Copper.
	- $Cu^{2+} + Zn \rightarrow Zn^{2+} + Cu$
- We can write a cell notation for it:
	- Zn | Zn<sup>2+</sup> || Cu<sup>2+</sup> | Cu
		- The LHS is the negative terminal - oxidation.
		- The RHS is the positive terminal - reduction.
		- The double line means a salt bridge.
- Then the electrons will need to be balanced.
- Then add the standard electrode potential for Cu<sup>2+</sup> to Cu and Zn to Zn<sup>2+</sup> together to yield the final electrode potential.
	- If the standard electrode potential is for Cu to Cu<sup>2+</sup> you should flip the sign of the electrode potential. We notate this as E<sub>cell</sub>.
> **NOTE** Salt bridges aren't always required. See the following example:
	- Pt | H<sub>2</sub> | H<sup>+</sup>, Cl<sup>-</sup> | AgCl | Ag
#### 2.3.3. Gibbs and E<sub>cell</sub>
- This is under standard conditions:
	- 298K
	- 1 bar
	- Activity
	- Concentration of all activity-determining ions = 1
- $\Delta_rG^o = -nFE^o$
	- n is the number of electrons in a half-cell equation.
	- F is Faraday constant
- If E is positive:
	- ΔG is negative
	- Spontaneous 
- If E is negative:
	- ΔG is positive
	- Non-spontaneous
> These cells are called **galvanic** cells.
	- There is no continuous flow of current
	- Only equilibrium cell potential from redox reactions
### 2.4. Electrodes
- There are four main types of electrodes:
	- Redox electrodes:
		- Inert electrode in solution of two ion redox species
		- Pt in Fe<sup>3+</sup> and Fe<sup>2+</sup>
	- Metal/metal electrodes:
		- Metal electrode in solution of ions of the same metal
		- Cu in Cu<sup>2+</sup>
	- Metal/insoluble electrode:
		- Metal coated with its insoluble salt, in a solution of the salt's anion.
		- Ag coated with AgCl in Cl<sup>-</sup>
		- **NOTE** The reaction is $AgCl + e^- \rightleftharpoons Ag + Cl^-$ 
			- Ag is reduced.
	- Gas electrodes:
		- Inert electrode which emits gas as a result of the reaction.
		- Pt in acids.
#### 2.4.1. The reference electrode
- As absolute electrode potentials cannot be measured, all electrode potentials are measured against the standard hydrogen electrode (SHE).
	- SHE is the electrode potential for:
		- $2H^+ + 2e^- \rightleftharpoons H_2$
	- It is given the value of 0.00 V.
#### 2.4.2. Oxidation and reduction
- Stronger oxidising agents will have a more positive electrode potential, e.g. F and Cl.
	- They really want to gain electrons.
#### 2.4.3. Measuring the electrode potential
- The setup involved:
	- SHE connected to negative, oxidising side of DVM
	- Measured electrode at positive, reducing side of DVM
	- Salt bridge in the middle
- Other electrodes are also used:
	- AgCl electrode
	- Saturated calomel electrode (SCE)
		- Hg | Hg<sub>2</sub>Cl<sub>2</sub> | Cl<sup>-</sup>
### 2.5. Electrochemical equations
- In the sections above we demonstrated how to obtain Gibbs and equilibrium constant form electrode potential.
- Similarly, we can obtain ΔS from this:\
	- From $dG = Vdp - SdT$
		- At constant pressure $dG = -SdT$
		- $(\frac{\partial G}{\partial T})_p = -S$
	- $\Delta_rS^o = nF(\frac{dE}{dT})$
> E<sup>o</sup> will typically be given as a quadratic or polynomial
#### 2.5.1. Nernst equation
- We can use the Nernst equation to predict E<sub>cell</sub> under non-standard conditions.
	- $E = E^0 - \frac{RT}{nF}\ln{(\frac{C^cD^c}{A^aB^b})}$
- For example, consider Ag and Cu.
	- Balanced equation is $2Ag + Cu^{2+} \rightleftharpoons 2Ag^+ + Cu$
	- $E = E^0 - \frac{RT}{2F}\ln{(\frac{(\alpha_{Ag^+})^2(\alpha_{Cu})}{(\alpha_{Ag})^2(\alpha_{Cu^{2+}})})}$
		- We use 2 for n because 2 electrons were transferred
		- The capital letters represent the activity of each species
		- The lower-case letters represent the stochiometric quantities of the species.
> **NOTE** For all solids the activity = 1, as they do not affect the solution.
- We can then say that Ag<sup>+</sup> and Cu<sup>2+</sup> are the rate-determining ions.
> **NOTE** You can calculate E for half-cells too.
