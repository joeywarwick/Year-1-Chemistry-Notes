## 2. Intro to acid-base chemistry (cont.)
- We know that after dissociation a certain amount of protons, and consequently H<sub>3</sub>O<sup>+</sup> will be generated.
	- As K<sub>a</sub> measures the extent of dissociation we can calculate pH from K<sub>a</sub>.
### 2.1. Determining pH
- We start my making two assumptions:
	- $[H_3O^+] = [A^-]$
		- Ignore contribution from ionisation of water
	- $[HA]_{final} = [HA]_{equilibrium}$
		- Ignore ionisation of HA
- We then consider 0.105 mol dm<sup>-3</sup> acetic acid in water, $K_a = 1.7 \times 10^{-5} \space mol \space dm^{-3}$
	- $K_a = \frac{[CH_3COO^-][H_3O^+]}{[CH_3COOH]} = \frac{[H_3O^+]^2}{0.105} =1.7 \times 10^{-5} \space mol \space dm^{-3}$
	- $[H_3O^+] = 1.336 \times 10^{-3} mol \space dm^{-3}$
	- pH = 2.874
### 2.2. Strength of bases
- We can consider the strength of bases in a similar manner.
	- $K_b = \frac{[BH^+][OH^-]}{[B]}$
- We can then calculate pK<sub>b</sub> accordingly.
### 2.3. Self-ionisation of water
- Water is amphoteric (see L6). A water molecule may act as a proton donor and another as proton acceptor.
	- $2H_2O \rightarrow OH^- + H_3O^+$
> **NOTE** Changes in [H<sub>2</sub>O] is negligible due to only a small proportion ionised.
- We have K<sub>w</sub> to quantify the degree of self-ionisation.
	- $K_w = [OH^-][H_3O^+] \space mol^2 \space dm^{-6}$
		- At 298K, $K_w = 1\times 10^{-14} \space mol^2\space dm^{-6}$
	- pK<sub>w</sub> = 14.
		- $\sqrt{K_w} = [OH^-] = [H_3O^+] = \times 10^{-7} \space mol\space dm^{-3}$
	- Water's pH is 7
- For a conjugate acids and bases, $K_aK_b = K_w$
	- $pK_w = pH + pOH$
### 2.4. Base stability
- Stabilising the base shifts equilibrium to the right, so more stable the base the more acidic the acid.
### 2.5. Polyprotic acids
- Polyprotic acids are acids with more than one protons, for example:
	- H<sub>2</sub>SO<sub>4</sub>
	- H<sub>2</sub>CO<sub>3</sub>
	- H<sub>3</sub>PO<sub>4</sub>
- They lose protons in succession, and therefore will have multiple K<sub>a</sub> values.
	- $K_{a_1} = \frac{[HSO_4^-][H_3O^+]}{[H_2SO_4]}$
	- $K_{a_2} = \frac{[SO_4^2-][H_3O^+]}{[HSO_4^-]}$
- Each successive K<sub>a</sub> becomes smaller.
	- This is because it is much harder to stabilise highly-charged species like SO<sub>4</sub><sup>2-</sup>.
## 3. Buffers
- Buffer solutions comprises a weak acid and its conjugate base.
	- It resists pH changes when small quantities of acid/base is added.
	- $HA + H_2O \rightarrow A^- + H_3O^+$
> Buffers work best when both the weak acid and the conjugate base are at the same high concentration (equimolar).
- When acid is added:
	- It reacts with A<sup>-</sup>, so its concentration decreases, but [HA] increases.
- When base is added:
	- It reacts with H<sub>3</sub>O<sup>+</sup>, but H<sub>3</sub>O<sup>+</sup> is replenished by [HA] decreasing, producing H<sub>3</sub>O<sup>+</sup> and A<sup>-</sup>. 
> It operates on Le Chatelier's.
- We can do calculations based around buffer to find it's pH too.
### 3.1. Buffer calculations
- We also make a few assumptions regarding buffers:
	- All A<sup>-</sup> ions come from the salt.
	- Weak acids remain as HA (due to low pK<Sub>a</sub> of the acid)
- We can now consider a weak acid HA.
	- $K_a = \frac{[A^-][H_3O^+]}{[HA]}$
	- If we do p on each side (-log), we get:
		- $pK_a = pH - \log{\frac{[A^-]}{[HA^-]}}$
	- If we further rearrange in terms of pH, we get:
		- $pH = pK_a + \log{\frac{[A^-]}{[HA^-]}}$
	- This is the **Henderson=Hasselbalch** equation/
- Remember we assume that A<sup>-</sup> comes entirely from the salt, so at 1:1 proportions pH = pK<sub>a</sub>.
- We can use this to adjust the buffer's pH.
	- pK<sub>a</sub> determines the range of pH the buffer would be in.
	- Acid-to-salt ratio can fine-tune the buffer pH.
### 3.2. Buffer applications
- Buffers are in our blood.
	- pH of the blood can determine the health of the patient.
## 4. Titrations
- Titrations between acids and bases are **neutralisation** reactions.
	- For a reaction between strong acid/base, end-point is pH = 7.
### 4.1. Titration curves
- The titration curve of a strong acid and strong base shows a vertical increase at pH = 7.
	- If we used lower concentrations of strong acids/bases, the curve would have a similar shape although more **squashed**.
- Between a weak acid and a strong base, the titration curve, compared to a strong acid and strong base, will:
	- Start at higher pH
	- Have a buffer region until half-way point where pH = pK<sub>a</sub>.
	- Slightly faster increase in pH until equivalence point, which is still vertical.
	- The equivalence point > 7.0 now.
	- Above the equivalence point the curve is same as strong acid-strong base.
### 4.2. Indicators
- The indicator should overlap with the steep vertical region of the titration curve.
- $K_{in} = \frac{[In^-][H_3O^+]}{[HIn]}$
	- Rearranging like Henderson-Hasselbalch:
		- $pH = pK_{In} + \log{\frac{[In^-]}{[HIn]}}$
- A colour change is visible when the ratio of In<sup>-</sup> to HIn changes greatly.
## 5. Solution conductivity
- Charged species will move due to charged voltage.
	- This is called **migration**.
	- The attraction between the ions and the electrodes can be quantified.
		- $F = ze\frac{V}{l}$
			- F is the force of attraction
			- z is the charge on ions
			- V is Voltage
			- l is separation (distance between the two electrodes)
- The voltage applied can be quantified with Ohm's law:
	- $V = IR$
		- V is voltage
		- I is current
		- R is resistance
	- $G = \frac{1}{R}$
		- G is conductance
		- R is resistance
	- So in fact R determines the conductance of the solution.
### 5.1. Resistance
- The resistance of the solution depends on four main factors:
	- Number of ions in solution
		- More ions, more charge(electron)-carrying species.
	- Cell geometry
	- Ion charge
		- Faster movement from stronger attraction
	- Ion speed
		- More current passed per unit time.
#### 5.1.1. Cell geometry
- With longer distance between electrodes:
	- Longer distance to travel for ions, increasing resistance.
		- $R \propto l$
- With larger electrode area, there is more area for ions to hit.
	- So more pathways available for current to flow.
		- $R\propto \frac{1}{A}$
		- $G \propto A$
- Combining these factors:
	- $R \propto \frac{l}{A}$
#### 5.1.2. Conductivity
- $K = \frac{1}{\rho} = \frac{L}{RA} = \frac{Gl}{A}$
	- ρ (rho) is resistivity.
- Conductivity (K) is conductance when we factor in the geometry of the cells.
	- It has units Ω<sup>-1</sup> m<sup>-1</sup>, or S cm<sup>-1</sup>.
> **NOTE** Conductance is S (siemens)
- So conductivity is essentially conductance when we factor in the impact of cell geometry on it.
- This is measured using a conductivity probe.
	- Its electrodes are placed at a fixed distance.
> Sometimes the electrodes may have corroded, resulting in different electrode area, so it must be calibrated.
	- Calibration is done by taking a solution of known **conductivity** and measuring that to determine the l/A term.
	- We can then measure resistance.
