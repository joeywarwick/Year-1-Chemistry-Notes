## 2. First-order reactions (cont.)
- We have a exponential decay curve to represent the concentration of A at a given time. 
	- $[A]_t = [A]_0e^{-kt}$
### 2.1. Half-life 
- Half-life of a reaction is notated as t<sub>1/2</sub>.
	- It is the time taken for the concentration of a reactant to halve.
 - Take $\ln{[A]_t} = \ln{[A]_0} - kt$
	 - $[A]_{t_\frac{1}{2}} = \frac{1}{2}[A]_0$
	 - $\ln{\frac{1}{2}} = -kt_{\frac{1}{2}}$
		- $-\ln{\frac{1}{2}} = \ln{2}$
	- $t_{\frac{1}{2}} = \frac{\ln{2}}{k}$
- Hence half-life for a first-order is a constant given the temperature.
	- So time for 1 to 0.5, from 0.5 to 0.25, and from 0.25 to 0.125 are all constant.
> Radioactive decay obeys first-order kinetics
## 3. Second-order reactions
- Most elementary reactions are second-order.
- They are simple to understand
	- Typically initiated by collisions between molecules.
- Consider the simplest second-order reaction below:
	- $A + A \rightarrow B$
		- e.g. Two methyl radical form ethane
> Because two atoms/molecules involved, known as bimolecular.
- Rate law is $Rate = -\frac{1}{2}\frac{d[A]}{dt} = k[A]^2$
### 3.1. Units of k
- Using the same method as L11 we can reach:
	- $Units = M^{-1}\space s^{-1} = dm^3 \space mol^{-1} \space s^{-1}$
### 3.2. Integrated rate equation for second-order
- $-\frac{1}{2}\frac{d[A]}{dt} = k[A]^2$
- $\int \frac{1}{[A]^2} d[A] = \int [A]^{-2} d[A] = -2k\int dt$
	- $\frac{1}{[A]_t} - \frac{1}{[A]_0} = 2kt$
- Rearranging again yields a straight line:
	- $\frac{1}{[A]_t} = \frac{1}{[A]_0} + 2kt$
- Rearranging for concentration of A directly gives:
	- $\frac{1}{[A]_t} = \frac{1+2kt[A]_0}{[A]_0}$
	- $[A]_t = \frac{[A]_0}{1+2kt[A]_0}$
### 3.3. Half-life
- We can determine half-life in a similar manner to first-order.
- This yields:
	- $t_{\frac{1}{2}} = \frac{1}{2k[A]_0}$
- We can see that half-life now is concentration dependent.
	- Essentially, it is inversely proportional to the initial concentration.
- As k is a constant, the half-life **doubles** for each successive half-life.
	- The half-life is hence not characteristic of the reaction and is not commonly used.
### 3.4. Complex second-order reactions
- There are cases where the two molecules reacting to form the product are not the same.
	- $A + B \rightarrow C$
- We obtain the rate law in a similar way:
	- $Rate = -\frac{d[A]}{dt} = -\frac{d{B}}{dt} = k[A][B]$
- The concentrations of both A and B change during the reaction, and the rate is dependent on both of them.
	- If $[A] \neq [B]$ then the rate law becomes complicated.
#### 3.4.1. Pseudo-first-order reactions
- We have seen that the kinetics for second-order reactions are challenging to model.
- To simplify, under certain conditions we can treat them as pseudo first-order reactions.
- This is usually done by making one of the reactants in **great excess**. 
	- Because we can then assume the concentration change is negligible in proportion.
- We take $Rate = k[A][B]$
	- Here **[B] is effectively a constant**.
	- So we write $Rate = k'[A]$
- k' is the **observed rate coefficient**.
	- It is correlated to the actual k, as above.
- Then the integrated rate law is now:
	- $\ln{[A]_t} = \ln_{[A]_0} - k't$
## 4. Zeroth-order reactions
- For some reactions the rate **appears** to be independent of the reactant concentration.
	- $Rate = k[A]^0 = k$
- The rate is given by k alone.
	- The units are therefore just $mol \space dm^{-3} \space s^{-1}$
- We can integrate this too:
	- $-\frac{d[A]}{dt} = k$
		- $\int d[A] = -k\int dt$
		- $[A]_t - [A]_0 = -kt$
	- $[A]_t = [A]_0 - kt$
- This gives a linear graph with gradient -k.
	- As k is the rate, -gradient = rate.
### 4.1. Half-life of zeroth-order reactions
- Substituting again yields $t_{\frac{1}{2}} = \frac{[A]_0}{2k}$
	- So each successive half-life is half of the length of the previous.
- As the half-life changes, it is **not** characteristic of the equation.
### 4.2. Existence of zeroth-order reactions
- If a reaction **appears** to be zeroth-order, it is **always** caused by the reaction conditions.
	- They are therefore referred to also as 'pseudo zeroth-order reactions'.
- Consider the thermal decomposition of N<sub>2</sub>O:
	- Under standard conditions it is second-order
	- When catalysed by Pt it can be saturated with N<sub>2</sub>O molecules.
		- A molecule needs to detach itself from the catalysts for the next one to bind.
		- This rate is **not** affected by concentration.
