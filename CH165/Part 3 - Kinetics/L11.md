## 2. Intro to kinetics
- Thermodynamics only tells us about the spontaneity of a reaction, but not how fast it will proceed.
	- Thermodynamics gives information about the energy change and the final state of the system, but not the speed of the change.
- Kinetics can tell us about the speed at which reactions occur.
### 2.1. Factors affecting reaction rates
- Reactions rates are affected by the following factors:
	- Concentration of reactants:
		- More reactants the higher chance of contact.
		- So higher chance of reactions.
		- Macroscopically more reactions in unit time.
	- Temperature:
		- Higher temperature provides more energy to reactions.
	- Polarity and viscosity of solvent.
	- Catalysts:
		- Catalysts provide alternate pathways that increase the rate.
### 2.2. Timescales
- Reactions can occur any timespan between femtoseconds (10<sup>-15</sup>) to petaseconds (10<sup>15</sup>).
### 2.3. Rate equation
- Kinetics can provide information on the mechanisms behinds a chemical change.
	- An apparently complex reaction can be broken down into a series of **elementary** equations.
- We can establish a rate equation that gives the rate as an expression of concentration:
	- $Rate = k[A][B]...$
#### 2.3.1. Rate coefficient
- The k in the above reaction is the rate coefficient. 
	- It tells us how fast a reaction occurs at a **given temperature**.
### 2.4. What is rate?
- The rate of reaction is the speed at which reactants are consumed or products are formed.
	- Units are **concentration/time** e.g. M s<sup>-1</sup>;
	- Or if in gas phase, **pressure/time** e.g. Pa s<sup>-1</sup>.
- Consider a reaction where a single reactant forms a single product.
	- The rate is not constant, and changes with time.
	- As the time progresses the rate decreases
	- At the end the rate plateaus.
#### 2.4.1. Formulating reactant rate
- The gradient of the curve of concentration against time is the rate.
	- We can determine it by drawing tangents at the desired time and determining the gradient of the tangent.
	- We can also differentiate the curve to find the gradient
 > **NOTE** The rate at the very beginning is important and is known as the **initial rate** of reaction.
- The rate is defined to be a positive quantity, but the gradient is decreasing.
	- $Rate = -\frac{dc_{reactant}}{dt}$
- In our simple reaction, the rate of product formation is the same as reactant consumption, so:
	- $Rate = -\frac{dc_{reactant}}{dt} = \frac{dc_{product}}{dt}$
#### 2.4.2. Stoichiometric factors
- We can consider a slightly more complex reaction:
	- $2H_2O_2 \rightarrow 2H_2O + O_2$
- For every two molecule of hydrogen peroxide that reacts, two molecules of water and a molecule of oxygen forms.
	- The rate of formation of oxygen is half of water's.
- We want to define the rate such that the rate is the same regardless of which product/reactants is used.
	- $Rate = -\frac{1}{2}\frac{d[H_2O_2]}{dt} = \frac{1}{2}\frac{d[H_2O]}{dt} = \frac{d[O_2]}{dt}$
- So the rate is half of the rate of the consumption of hydrogen peroxide
## 3. Measuring kinetics
- We measure and study kinetics by considering the concentration of reactants and their change in time.
- So we need to:
	- Start reaction
	- Measure concentration as function of time
	- Control temperature
### 3.1. Measuring concentrations
#### 3.1.1. Chemical methods
- For slow reactions we can use titrations.
- For example, consider the reaction between bromoethane and sodium hydroxide:
	- $C_2H_5Br + OH^- \rightarrow C_2H_5OH + Br^-$
	- This reaction is slow enough for us to take a small sample (aliquot) of the reaction mixture and titrate it with an acid
		- This allows us to determine the concentration of hydroxide ions in the mixture.
	- This method has negative though:
		- Collecting the sample may disturb the reaction mixture
		- Time is required to perform titration so the aliquot need to be quenched.
#### 3.1.2. Physical methods - volume
- Physical methods are often better because they allow the concentrations to be measured in a non-intrusive way.
- Consider a neutralisation reaction between sodium and hydrochloric acid:
	- $2Na + 2HCl \rightarrow 2NaCl + H_2$
	- We can measure the volume of hydrogen evolved at a given time to determine rate.
#### 3.1.3. Physical methods - mass
- Consider the same reaction.
	- If the reaction was carried out in an open container H<sub>2</sub> escapes, decreasing mass.
	- Measuring mass of the reaction mixture at a given time can also be used to determine rate.
#### 3.1.4. Physical methods - spectroscopy
- A wide range of spectroscopic techniques can be used:
	- UV-Vis
	- IR
	- NMR
- They all measure absorbance, A.
	- The absorbance is proportional to the concentration of a solution.
> **NOTE** This comes from the Beer-Lambert law, $A = \epsilon cl$
	- e (epsilon) is the extinction coefficient
	- c is the concentration
	- l is the length which the light travels.
- A special case is UV-Vis.
	- It is frequently used when a reactant is coloured.
	- If the colour changes as it proceeds, we can measure the change in absorbance with regard to time to study rate.
- NMR is used by measuring how the hydrogen signal changes through the reaction.
#### 3.1.5. Physical methods - conductivity
- If the number/type of ions change in the reaction, conductivity measurements can be used to determine the rate.
- Similarly, a glass electrode which only detects H<sup>+</sup> can be used.
	- To measure change in pH
#### 3.1.6. Physical methods - MS
- Mass spectrometry (MS) detects molecules by ionising them and detecting their m/z ratios.
- This is a very sensitive technique.
- Often used when isotope ratios change.
	- Example is carbon dating to determine the concentration of <sup>14</sup>C.
### 3.2. Initiation of the reaction
- There are many ways to which reactions can start, but the initiation method needs to be suitable for the timescale of the reaction.
- The most common method is just mixing everything together.
	- This is viable for slower reactions that occur over minutes/hours.
- For faster reactions other methods are required.
	- Common methods are flow techniques and photolysis
#### 3.2.1. Stopped flow method
- Stopped flow method is suitable for timescales as short as miliseconds (ms).
- Syringes containing reactants are pressurised, forcing them into a mixing chamber that mixes everything in ~1ms.
- After a certain volume has been added the plunger of the third syringe stops and the flow is halted.
- The concentration is then monitored using techniques like spectroscopy.
#### 3.2.2. Flash photolysis
- Reactions occurring in timeframes shorter than ms can be studied using flash photolysis.
- Typically, a flash of light breaks down the precursor molecule.
- The system is then probed at pre-defined intervals of time.
## 4. Elementary reactions
- An elementary reaction typically has the following characteristics:
	- Single-step, no intermediates
	- Between 1 or 2 reactants
		- **Very rarely > 2**
	- The chemical reaction represents the actual changes on a molecular level
		- Chemical equation can be directly used to write the rate equation
> **NOTE** All reactions can be broken down into elementary reactions.
### 4.1. Rate equations
- For elementary reactions in the form of:
	- $aA + bB \rightarrow Products$
- $Rate = -\frac{1}{a}\frac{d[A]}{dt} = -\frac{1}{b}\frac{d[B]}{dt} = k[A]^a[B]^b$
	- The stoichiometric coefficients give the reaction order.
		- a + b = reaction order.
> **NOTE** Most elementary reactions are 1st or 2nd order.
> **NOTE** The units of k change with order
### 4.2. First-order reactions
- First order reactions have a single molecule going to a product.
	- They are (fittingly) named unimolecular reactions.
- For unimolecular reactions, $Rate = k[A]$
	- So $Rate \propto [A]$
#### 4.2.1. Units of first-order reactions
> **NOTE** This section presents a general method of working out the units for orders of reaction. Memorisation of units is hence not required.
- We know that the rate can we written in the form of $k[A]$.
	- Rearranging gives $k = \frac{Rate}{[A]}$
	- $k = \frac{M \space s^{-1}}{M}$
	- $k = s^{-1}$
	- So the units of k for first-order reactions is s<sup>-1</sup>.
#### 4.2.2. Integrated rate equation
- The rate equations are capable of telling us the rate at a given time.
- The experimental data requires us to draw tangents to extract a value of k.
	- This process is time-consuming and can be inaccurate.
- Therefore, for these elementary reactions it is easier in general to work with integrated rate equations.
- Consider the first-order reaction $A \rightarrow B$.
	- $Rate = -\frac{d[A]}{dt} = k[A]$
		- $\int \frac{1}{[A]} d[A] = -k\int dt$
		- $\ln{\frac{[A]_t}{[A]_0}} = -kt$
- We can convert this into a straight-line format.
	- $\ln{[A]_t} = \ln{[A]_0} - kt$
		- So this gives a linear graph with gradient -k
	- $[A]_t = [A]_0e^{-kt}$
		- This gives a exponential decay curve of A's concentration.
