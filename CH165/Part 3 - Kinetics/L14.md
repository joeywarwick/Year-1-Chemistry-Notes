## 2. Reaction mechanisms
- We can propose a mechanism using kinetics.
	- The mechanism is essentially the sequence of elementary equations that lead to the products from the reactants.
		- Every elementary step has its own rate law and k, which contributes to the overall k and rate law.
		- The time scales can be very different; a overall slow reaction can be the result of many fast reactions.
- A **proposed** mechanism can be used to predict the rate law.
	- If the proposed rate law is different from experimental rate law then the proposed mechanism is **wrong**.
	- If the proposed rate law is concordant with experimental rate law this gives confidence in the mechanism, but it musl aos **account for other factors**:
		- Product distribution/Stereochemistry
		- Temperature dependence
		- Kinetic isotope effect
### 2.1. Proposing rate laws from reaction mechanisms
- Consider the reaction $A \rightarrow P$
	- It involves an intermediate I, so a possible mechanism is $A \rightarrow I \rightarrow P$
	- Each individual step has its own k, so the rate laws would be:
		- $R_1 = k_1[A]$
		- $R_2 = k_2[I]$
> This type of sequential reactions are often found in atmospheric reactions and radioactive decay.
- From here we can write the rate laws for each of the three species invovled:
	- $\frac{d[A]}{dt} = -k_1[A]$
	- $\frac{d[I]}{dt} = k_1[A]-k_2[I]$
	- $\frac{d[P]}{dt} = k_2[I]$
> **NOTE** The rate law for P is not an acceptable rate law - intermediates do not appear in reactions
- By integrating them and substituting we can eventually reach an expression for [P]. But it is **very complicated**.
### 2.2. Rate-determining steps
- If one of the steps is far slower than any others, it becomes the bottleneck and hence controls the overall rate of reaction.
- This step is known as the **rate-determining** step.
- Consider the reaction $A \rightarrow I \rightarrow P$ again:
	- If the second step is much slower than the first it becomes the RDS
		- So $k_2 < k_1$
	- We can then ignore k<sub>2</sub> as it becomes negligible.
	- Simplifying the expression results in $[P]_t = [A]_0(1 - e^{-k_2t})$
- The simplified expression closely resembles the analytical solution.
- Similarly, if $k_1 < k_2$
	- $[P]_t = [A]_0(1 - e^{-k_1t})$
> If a complex reaction comprises of only first-order steps we can always reach an analytical solution.
- For mechanisms that contain reaction other than first-order (most of them), it must be numerically integrated with a computer.
- We can also use approximations under certain conditions.
	- RDS is one of the approximations
	- Other approximations include:
		- Pre-equilibria
		- Steady-state (more general)
### 2.3. Pre-equilibria
- Consider the reaction $A + B \rightleftharpoons C \rightarrow D$
	- If we notate the forward reaction of $A + B \rightleftharpoons C$ as k<sub>1</sub>, the backwards reaction k<sub>-1</sub>, and the second step as k<sub>2</sub>:
		- $\frac{d[A]}{dt} = \frac{d[B]}{dt} = -k_1[A][B]$
		- $\frac{d[C]}{dt} = k_1[A][B] - k_2[C]$
		- $\frac{d[D]}{dt} = k_2[C]$
	- Like above, the rate equation for D is not accepted, as C is an intermediate.
- If k<sub>2</sub> is much smaller than k<sub>-1</sub>, A and B will be able to equilibrate with C.
	- Even though C is being removed, it is happening much slower than the equilibration between A, B, and C.
- Kinetically, an equilibrium is established when the forward and backward reactions occur at the same rate.
	- So at equilibrium:
		- $k_1[A][B] = k_{-1}[C]$
		- $\frac{[C]}{[A][B]} = \frac{k_1}{k_{-1}} = K_c$
	- The equilibrium constant is the ratio between the rate coefficients of the forward and backward reactions.
- Using this we can write a rate equation for D in terms of A and B.
	- $[C] = K_c[A][B] = \frac{k_1}{k_{-1}}[A][D]$
	- So $\frac{d[D]}{dt} = k_2K_c[A][B] = k'[A][B]$
- Overall the order is 2nd order.
> **NOTE** This only holds after the equilibrium has been established.
