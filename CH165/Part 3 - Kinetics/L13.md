## 2. Activation energy
- The rates of most elementary equations increase as the temperature increases.
	- k depends on temperature.
### 2.1. Arrhenius equation
- The Arrhenius equation models the temperature factor on the value of k.
	- $k = Ae^{\frac{E_a}{RT}}$
		- A is the pre-exponential factor, and has the same units as k.
#### 2.1.1. Graphical representations
- The Arrhenius tells us that the rate coefficient (k) increases exponentially with temperature.
- If we take the natural logarithms of both sides of the equation:
	- $\ln{k} = ln{A} - \frac{E_a}{RT}$
- We can see that a plot of ln k 1/T gives a straight line with gradient -E<sub>a</sub>/R and intercept ln A.
### 2.2. The activation energy
- The activation energy determines the sensitivity of the reaction to temperature.
	- A large activation energy means very sensitive to T
	- A small activation energy means insensitive to T.
- By taking expressions for k at two separate temperatures:
	- $\ln{k_1} = ln{A} - \frac{E_a}{RT_1}$
	- $\ln{k_2} = ln{A} - \frac{E_a}{RT_2}$
- We subtract to give
	- $\ln{\frac{k_2}{k_1}} = \frac{E_a}{R}(\frac{1}{T_1}-\frac{1}{T_2})$
#### 2.2.1. Reaction profiles
- A reaction profile is used to depict changes in potential energy through the course of the reaction.
- Typically, even for exothermic reactions, the energy tends to increase before decreasing again.
	- This is because bonds typically need to be broken before rearranged to form the product.
- The highest point is called the **transition state**.
- The energy difference between the transition state and the reactants is the activation energy.
## 3. Collision theory
- Consider a simple bimolecular reaction:
	- $A + B \rightarrow C$
- In order for this to take place, a few conditions must be met:
	- Thermodynamically favourable
	- Reactant molecules collide
	- Kinetic energy of collision > activation energy.
### 3.1. Kinetic energy
- The kinetic energy is (obviously) linked to the speed of the molecules.
	- $E_k = \frac{1}{2}mv^2$
- Consider a gas:
	- Different molecules move at varying speeds.
- This distribution is visualised by the **Maxwell-Boltzmann** distribution.
	- As temperature increases, the distribution become wider.
	- This is because higher-temperature = more kinetic energy
		- So more collisions have enough energy to overcome the reaction barrier.
	- The fraction of collisions with sufficient energy can be shown to be equal to $e^{\frac{E_a}{RT}}$
### 3.2. The pre-exponential factor
- Looking at the Arrhenius again:
	- $k = Ae^{\frac{E_a}{RT}}$
- The pre-exponential factor in Arrhenius
	- Would be 1 if all molecules could collide
- So A is related to the rate of collision between molecules.
> **NOTE** There are other factors to take into account, e.g. orientation of collision. This is a very rudimentary treatment.
## 4. Complex reactions
- Complex reactions are combinations of different elementary reactions.
- Complex reactions:
	- Comprises more than one elementary step and one or more intermediates.
	- Rate equation cannot be derived from the chemical equations
		- Must be derived from experimental data
### 4.1. Intermediates and transition states
- Each step of a complex reaction will have a transition state.
	- They are not stable molecules and cannot be detected spectroscopically.
- Intermediates are molecules that are formed and consumed again during a reaction.
	- They do not appear in rate equations
	- They are somewhat stable and can exist for some time during the reactions
	- Detectable spectroscopically
### 4.2. Rate equations for complex reactions
- The rate equation follows a similar format to elementary equations.
- Consider the reaction $aA + bB \rightarrow Product$
	- $Rate = k[A]^x[B]^y$
	- Here k is the **overall rate coefficient**.
> **NOTE** a and b are not necessarily equal to x and y!
- They can have a **fraction** as the overall reaction order.
	- Order still given by x + y
- The rate equation can include molecules from things like products and catalysts, but not **intermediates**.
### 4.3. Determining the order
- We can determine the reaction order using the isolation method.
	- Similar to pseudo first-order reactions, we assume all other reactants are in great excess.
	- So we can write a pseudo rate law.
- Repeating this method we can obtain a pseudo rate law for all reactants.
- Piecing them together can yield a overall rate law.
- Consider the reaction $A + B \rightarrow Product$
	- We can run two sets of experiments:
		- B in great excess:
			- $Rate = k'_B[A]^x$
		- A in great excess:
			- $Rate = k'_A[B]^y$
- We can use this in conjunction with the integrated rate laws:
	- We test which rate law plot results in a straight line to determine the order of the reaction
#### 4.3.1. Alternative methods - initial rates
- Sometimes the isolation method may not be viable:
	- A reactant can be very expensive
	- A reactant is dangerous in large quantities
- So alternate methods must be used.
- We can use the **initial rates** method.
	- We determine the rate at t = 0 using a tangent.
		- Here the rate is determined by initial rate.
	- Here $R_0 = k[A]^x_0[B]^y_0$
	- By varying the values of the initial concentration, the rate changes, and we can determine x and y.
- Consider the following data:

| [A] | [B] | R<sub>0</sub> |
| --- | --- | ------------- |
| 1   | 1   | 1             |
| 1   | 2   | 2             |
| 2   | 1   | 4             |
| 2   | 2   | 8             |
- From this we know:
	- x would be 2 (rate quadruples when A doubles)
	- y would be 1 (rate doubles when B doubles.)
- Sometimes inspection of the data is insufficient:
	- Rate increases by non-integer multiples
	- Non-integer orders
- Here we can use an **analytical** approach.
- Consider the rate law $R_0 = k[A]^x_0[B]^y_0$ again:
	- If we take logs on both sides we can yield:
		- $\ln{R_0} = \ln{k} + x\ln{[A]}+ y\ln{[B]}$
	- We can now inspect the data and compare values.
