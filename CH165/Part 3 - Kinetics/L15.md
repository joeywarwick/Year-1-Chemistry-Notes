## 2. Reaction mechanisms (cont.)
- Another method to approximate the rate equation is the **steady-state** approximation.
### 2.1. Steady-state approximation
- A reaction can often involve a intermediate that is highly reactive, e.g. free radicals.
	- They react as soon as they form, and as such, their concentrations will remain at a low level that is near constant.
- Consider $A + B \rightleftharpoons C \rightarrow D$ again.
	- If C is very reactive, we can say:
		- $\frac{d[C]}{dt} = (R_{formation_I} - R_{consumption_I}) \approx 0$
	- We begin by estimating the concentration of C by setting its rate of formation to 0.
		- $\frac{d[C]}{dt} = k_1[A][B] - k_{-1}[C] - k_2[C] = 0$
	- Then we can say:
		- $k_1[A][B] = (k_{-1}+k_2)[C]$
	- Rearranging:
		- $[C] = \frac{k_1}{k_{-1}+k_2}[A][B]$
	- Substituting gives:
		- $\frac{d[D]}{dt} = \frac{k_1k_2}{k_{-1}+k_2}[A][B]$
- There are two limiting cases:
	- Where k<sub>-1</sub> is far greater than k<sub>1</sub> and k<sub>2</sub>,
		- $\frac{d[D]}{dt} = k_2k_c[A][B]$
		- Here a pre-equilibrium occurs but C is very small anyways.
	- Where k<sub>2</sub> is far greater than k<sub>1</sub> and k<sub>-1</sub>.
		- $\frac{d[D]}{dt} = k_1[A][B]$
	- Essentially here the first step is the RDS.
> We can see that RDS and Pre-equilibrium approximations are simplified cases of SS approximation.
## 3. Catalysis
- Catalysts increase the rate of reactions without being consumed.
	- They do not appear in the chemical equation but may appear in the rate law.
> They do not affect the **thermodynamics** of the reaction.
- It only affects the kinetic aspect of a reaction.
- They provide alternate pathways with a lower E<sub>a</sub>.
	- From Arrhenius we know that lower E<sub>a</sub> means greater rate at a given temperature.
- They are either homogenous or heterogenous.
	- Homogenous catalysts are in the same phase as the reactants.
	- Heterogenous catalysts are in a different phase.
		- Many industrial processes involve heterogenous catalysts
		- The reactant mixture will adsorb onto the surface and react before desorbing.
			- Rate laws are separate for each of these two processes.
		- They are easier to remove.
### 3.1. Enzymes
- Enzymes are biological homogenous catalysts.
	- They catalyse many important biological processes.
#### 3.1.1. Induced fit
- Enzymes interact with the target molecule (substrate) at its active site.
	- They have similar shapes
- Once bound, the enzyme reshapes the site, changing the shape of the substrate to be closer to the transition state
	- Hence lowering E<sub>a</sub>.
#### 3.1.2. Kinetics of enzymes
- From experiments we know that:
	- At low substrate concentrations reactions are first-order with respect to substrate.
	- At high substrate concentrations reactions are zeroth-order with respect to substrate.
#### 3.1.3. Michaelis-Menten equation
- Michaelis and Menten proposed the following mechanism for enzyme-catalysed reactions:
	- $E + S \rightleftharpoons ES \rightarrow E + P$
		- k<sub>1</sub> for forward reaction to ES
		- k<sub>-1</sub> for backwards reaction to E and S
		- k<sub>2</sub> to E + P
- Applying previous approximations, the rate is given by:
	- $\frac{d[P]}{dt} = k_2[ES]$
- We assume that ES is short-lived, so we can use the steady-state approximation:
	- $\frac{d[ES]}{dt} = k_1[E][S] - k_{-1}[ES] - k_2[ES] \approx 0$
		- $k_1[E][S] = (k_{-1}+k_2)[ES]$
	- We know that E is not consumed, so:
		- $[E] = [E]_0 - [ES]$
			- Where [E]<sub>0</sub> is the initial concentration of free, unbound enzymes.
	- So we can now write:
		- $(k_{-1}+k_2)[ES] = k_1[E]_0[S] - k_1[ES][S]$
		- $(k_{-1}+k_2+k_1[S])[ES] = k_1[E]_0[S]$
	- Dividing both sides by k<sub>1</sub>
		- $(\frac{(k_{-1}+k_2)}{k_1}+[S])[ES] = [E]_0[S]$
	- Overall:
		- $[ES] = \frac{[E]_0[S]}{K_m+[S]}$
			- We define $K_m = \frac{k_{-1}+k_2}{k_1}$
	- The rate of P formation is therefore:
		- $\frac{d[P]}{dt} = k_2[ES]$
	- If we define $V_{max} = k_2[E]_0$
		- $\frac{d[P]}{dt} = \frac{V_{max}[S]}{K_m+[S]}$
- This yields the **Michaelis-Menten equation**.
> **NOTE** K<sub>m</sub> is **not** an equilibrium constant, it is the Michaelis constant.
	- K<sub>m</sub> yields a concentration.
- The M-M equation agrees with experimental observations:
	- At low [S], $K_m + [S] \approx K_m$
		- $Rate = \frac{V_{max}[S]}{K_m}$
	- At high [S], $K_m + [S] \approx [S]$
		- $Rate =V_{max}$
#### 3.1.4. V<sub>max</sub>
- V<sub>max</sub> is the maximum rate of reaction.
	- It occurs in very high substrate concentrations such that $[ES] = [E]_0$
	- Because all enzymes are saturated, rate is independent of [S]
#### 3.1.5. K<sub>m</sub>
- K<sub>m</sub> is the Michaelis constant.
	- It is the concentration required for $Rate = \frac{1}{2} V_{max}$
		- Small K<sub>m</sub> means high affinity, rate approaches maximum more readily.
	- K<sub>m</sub> is dependent on both the conditions and the substrate-enzyme pair.
