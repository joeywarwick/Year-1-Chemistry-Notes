## 2. Bromination of alkenes
- The reaction between alkenes and bromines use CCl<sub>4</sub> as the solvent.
	- Alkene is the nucleophile
	- Bromine is the electrophile.
- The reaction goes through a bromonium ion intermediate.
	1. The bromine is attacked with the π-electrons from the C=C bond.
	2. The bromine in turn attacks the other end of the C=C bond, forming the bromonium ion.
	3. The other bromide ion attacks the bromonium ion
	4. Dibromoalkane is formed.
### 2.1. Reactivity of alkenes
- With EDGs such as more alkyl groups, they raise the energy of the HOMO of C=C with hyperconjugation
	- Smaller energy gap between LUMO and HOMO decreases activation energy
	- So faster reactions.
### 2.2. Stereochemistry of bromination
- The bromines can undergo syn, or anti addition.
	- syn refers to addition on the same side.
	- anti is addition of different sides.
- Consider a bromonium ion.
	- The C-Br bonds must be on the same side.
	- To avoid steric hindrance, addition occurs from the other side
	- Another reason is this can accidentally attack bromine, resulting in regeneration of molecular bromine.'
- So anti-addition occurs.
> **NOTE** Bromination retains the geometric stereochemistry of the alkene used. So the conformers are similar in stereochemistry to the geometric isomers.
### 2.3. Ring-opening with other nucleophiles
- If the bromonium cation is attacked with other nucleophiles they can be added too.
	- In a mixture of water and bromine, bromoalcohol forms.
	- In a mixture of Bromine and methanol a bromoether forms.
	- In a mixture of bromine and a carboxylic acid a bromoester forms.
