## 2. Alkenes
- Alkenes are characterised by a C=C double bond.
	- The double bond is formed between two sp<sup>2</sup> carbon centres, via the side overlap of two p-orbitals.
### 2.1. Geometrical isomerism
- In part 1, we established that the rotational barrier around a π-bond causes geometrical isomerism.
- Geometrical isomers can have different properties.
- Consider cis-but-2-ene and trans-but-2-ene.
	- Cis has a bp of 4 degrees
	- Trans has a bp of 1 degree.
- The isomers have varying bp due to dipoles.
	- In cis, the two bonds add constructively to give an overall dipole.
	- In trans, the two bonds add destructively to cancel out each other and yield no dipole.
> **NOTE** Between carbons, bonds are polarised towards sp<sup>2</sup>, because it is lower in energy.
#### 2.1.1. Fatty acids
- Fatty acids decrease in mp with the increase in number of C=C bonds.
	- The bonds are typically cis-bonds.
- This is because a regular zigzag shape would permit good intermolecular interactions.
	- This is only continued by trans C=C bonds.
### 2.2. Acidity of alkenes and alkynes
- Again, we can assess the acidity using pK<sub>a</sub>.
	- Consider the carbanions:
		- <sup>-</sup>CH<sub>2</sub>Me, pK<sub>a</sub> = 50
		- CH<sub>2</sub>=CH<sup>-</sup>, pK<sub>a</sub> = 44 (vinyl anion)
		- <sup>-</sup>CH<sub>2</sub>CH=CH<sub>2</sub>, pK<sub>a</sub> = 43 (allyl anion)
	- Carbanions in sp<sup>2</sup> like alkenes are more stable.
		- The negative charge can delocalise across the C=C bond to stabilise the anion via conjugation.
