## 2. Reduction of alkenes
- We can reduce alkenes to alkanes by reacting it with hydrogen under a metal catalysts, usually palladium (Pd).
	- They use a solid support to maximise surface area, usually charcoal or calcium carbonate.
> Pd deposited on CaCO<sub>3</sub> is also known as Lindlar's catalyst.
### 2.1. Procedure
> **NOTE** No mechanism required.
- The protons bind to the surface of the metal catalyst.
- The alkene is then coordinated to the surface
- The proton is then transferred onto the alkene.
#### 2.1.1. Chemoselectivity
- The amount of hydrogen and the pressure of hydrogen can affect the course of the reaction.
- At lower pressures:
	- The **less** substituted C=C is reduced
		- This is due to steric hindrance.
- Heating and pressurising to 70 degrees and 200 atm:
	- Both are reduced.
## 3. Reduction of alkynes
- The hydrogenation of alkyne to alkene occurs quite quickly even at RT.
	- The conditions are the same, Pd/C.
- The alkene will be further reduced to alkanes, although at a slower rate.
## 3.1. Reduction to alkene only
####  3.1.1. Catalyst poisons
- To retain the alkene, we need to carry out the reaction in slightly different conditions.
	- We can poison a catalyst by using lead acetate and quinoline.
	- They decrease the efficiency of the metal catalysts.
	- So we can isolate the alkene.
- We can produce **cis**-alkenes in this condition due to only one side contacting the metal catalyst.
> **NOTE** The reduction to alkane still occurs, albeit much slower.
#### 3.1.2. Trans-alkenes
- By adding lithium and ammonium at -33 degrees, the lithium will be solvated in the liquid ammonia.
	- This yields solvated lithium cations and solvated electrons.
> This is the Birch condition.
## 4. Reduction of aromatics
- Consider an ester with an aromatic ring.
	- When we add LiAlH<sub>4</sub> with an acid workup we yield a primary alcohol, with the aromatic ring intact.
	- If we pass hydrogen at 100 atm over nickel catalyst, we reduce the aromatic ring, leaving the ester group intact.
- Consider a nitrobenzene.
	- If we passed hydrogen at RT over Lindlar's catalyst, we get aniline (aminobenzene)
### 4.1. Hydrogenolysis
- We can break benzylic C-O and C-N bonds as such.
	- They might be useful because benzyl groups act as protecting groups.
	- In a benzyl alcohol, it can protect the hydroxy group such that reactions can be done with the hydroxy group.
	- The benzyl group can be later removed using methods like this yielding the desired molecule.
- Reacting PhCH<sub>2</sub>OR with hydrogen under Lindlar's yields a molecule of toluene and the alcohol, ROH.
- Reacting PhCH<sub>2</sub>NR<sub>2</sub> with hydrogen under Lindlar's yields a molecule of toluene and R<sub>2</sub>NH, the amine.
#### 4.1.1. Reactivity of double and triple bonds 
- Consider the following molecules:

| Molecule       | Reduced to        | Reactivity |
| -------------- | ----------------- | ---------- |
| Benzene        | Cyclohexane       | Least      |
| Nitrile        | Amine             |            |
| Benzyl alcohol | Toluene + Alcohol |            |
| Alkene         | Alkane            |            |
| Alkyne         | Alkene            |            |
| Nitrobenzene   | Phenylamine       |            |
| Acid chlorides | Alcohol           | Most       |
> **NOTE** Non-examinable.
## 5. Hydration of alkynes
- We know that we can make alcohols from alkenes in aqueous solution of acid.
- Alkynes form a enol in the same condition
	- Enols have a keto form, so we are also forming ketones via this reaction.
- This reaction fundamentally increases the oxidation **level** by 2 (See L18).
