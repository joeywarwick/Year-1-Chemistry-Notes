## 2. Nucleophiles and electrophiles

| Nucleophiles                                         | Electrophiles                                       |
| ---------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------- |
| Donates electrons to from a bond with a electrophile | Accepts electrons to form a bond with a nucleophile |
| Nu<sup>-</sup>, Nu:                                  | E<sup>+</sup>                                       |

| Bronsted Base                                                      | Bronsted Acid                                   |
| ------------------------------------------------------------------ | ----------------------------------------------- |
| A reagent that accepts a proton by donating both bonding electrons | A reagent that donates a proton to form a bond. |

- So nucleophiles are often Bronsted bases, and electrophiles are often Bronsted acids.
### 2.1. Orbitals
- When a Nu and a E react, the HOMO of th Nu overlaps with the LUMO of the E.
- The specific HOMO and LUMO can help in understanding reactions.
## 3. Common types of reactions
- There are a few types of common reactions
### 3.1. Nucleophilic substitution
- A nucleophile attacks a electrophile and replaces one of the groups.
- Consider Benzoyl chloride reacting with methylamine.
	- Do we break the π-bond of C=O or do we break the C-Cl bond?
	- We need to consider the orbital.
		- The LUMO of the electrophile is π*, so we must break the π-bond so it can overlap with the LP on methylamine.
	- So the final product is methylbenzamide.
	- Cl<sup>-</sup> is ejected
### 3.2. Nucleophilic addition
- A nucleophile attacks a electrophile **without** ejecting a leaving group.
- Consider Acetone with sodium cyanide
	- After the π-bond is broken the O is not ejected.
	- Instead it is quenched with H<sup>+</sup>
	- This yields a hydroxynitrile.
### 3.3. Electrophilic addition
- The nucleophile attacks a electrophile, generating a nucleophile and a electrophile.
- The electrophile and nucleophile add onto the original nucleophile.
- Consider ethene with HBr.
	- The π-electrons in ethene attack the hydrogen as it has a partially positive charge.
	- So forms +CH<sub>2</sub>-CH<sub>3</sub>.
	- The Br<sup>-</sup> attacks to form bromoethane.
> **NOTE** This is electrophilic addition because an electrophile was added.
### 3.4. Electrophilic substitution
- A nucleophile attacks an electrophile, ejecting a leaving group.
- Consider benzene and nitronium ion.
	- The π-electrons in benzene attack the nitronium ion, forming a carbocation.
	- The hydrogen on the same carbon as the nitro group is ejected after adding a base.
> **NOTE** The base do not add to the carbocation. This is because the resulting molecule is not aromatic and is therefore not stable
> **NOTE** The base also does not attack the carbocation hydrogen. This results in carbon with 6 valence electron (carbene) and is high-energy.
## 4. Selectivity
- Selectivity describes why reactions typically only progress down one pathway.
- We can assess this based on reactivity rules (See L7).
### 4.1. Origin of electrons.
#### 4.1.1. Electronegativity
- When selecting an atom as the starting point, the **less electronegative**, **the more reactive**.
	- This is because nucleophiles need to donate a pair of electrons.
	- Lower electronegativity makes it easier for electrons to be lost.
- Similar case with LPs
	- Thiols (R-SH) are more nucleophilic than alcohols (R-OH). Sulfur is less electronegative compared to oxygen.
#### 4.1.2. Mesomeric effect
- Resonance stabilises electrons by spreading it out.
- So the greater extent of conjugation the weaker the nucleophile.
#### 4.1.3. Inductive effect
- EDGs also increase the nucleophilicity by donating some electron density.
- It can often work constructively with mesomeric effects too.
#### 4.1.4. Steric effects
- Smaller nucleophiles are typically more nucleophilic.
	- Their electrons are less sterically hindered, so are more accessible and easier to act as nucleophiles.
> **NOTE** More basic does not mean more nucleophilic. tert-butyl alkoxide is very basic (3 +I effects), but it is more hindered than methylalkoxide (1 +I effect).
