## 2. S<sub>N</sub>1
- Akin to S<sub>N</sub>2, S<sub>N</sub>1 is named in a similar manner:
	- Substitution
	- Nucleophilic
	- **Unimolecular** in the first step.
- S<sub>N</sub>1 proceeds in two steps.
	- First step involves the formation of a carbocation on the carbon.
		- This is done via heterolytic bond breaking to eject a leaving group.
	- Second step involves the attack on the positive charge by the nucleophile.
- Each of these steps have their own transition state, which have a partial positive charge.
### 2.1. Energetics of S<sub>N</sub>1
- This forms a reactive intermediate, so there is two transition states and a intermediate, forming a 'M-shaped' reaction profile.
### 2.2. Kinetics of S<sub>N</sub>1
- The formation of the carbocation is the RDS. Hence the rate equation is:
	- $Rate = k[CX]$
#### 2.2.1. Other factors affecting rate
- The other rate determining factors for nucleophiles are the same as S<sub>N</sub>2.
#### 2.2.2. Other factors affecting rate - electrophiles
- Substitution at the electrophilic carbon can affect rate.
	- Tertiary > Secondary > Primary...
		- This is because alkyl groups are EDGs so they stabilise the carbocation intermediate.
		- Other EDGs (-OH, -SH, etc.) can also increase rate.
> **NOTE** This is opposite to S<sub>N</sub>2.
> **NOTE** Rates are affected by the **transition** state. We use carbocation intermediates to **infer** how the TSs are affected.
- Mesomeric effects can also stabilise the transition state.
	- Larger conjugated systems offer greater stabilisation.
	- Hyperconjugation can also help to stabilise.
> **NOTE** Like S<sub>N</sub>2, S<sub>N</sub>1 does not occur with sp<sup>2</sup> carbons (e.g. Benzenes)
### 2.3. Stereochemistry of S<sub>N</sub>1
- The carbocation intermediate is sp<sup>2</sup>, meaning that its planar.
	- So the Nu can attack from either side of the carbocation
	- So the final product is typically racemic.
## 3. Solvents and S<sub>N</sub> reactions
- Solvents have a massive effect on the mechanism chosen and the rate.
### 3.1. S<sub>N</sub>1
- Polar protic solvents (water, alcohols) favour S<sub>N</sub>1 and increases the rate with increasing polarity, as they can stabilise both of the ions formed at intermediate by solvation.
	- They can form hydrogen bonds to the leaving group (anion)
- There is no reaction in apolar solvents.
### 3.2. S<sub>N</sub>2
- Polar aprotic solvents (DMF, Acetone) favour S<sub>N</sub>2 reactions, because the by-product e.g. NaCl, NaBr, etc. are not soluble in them. 
- They are quite slow in polar protic solvents.
	- Polar protic solvents quench the nucleophile's negative charge partially.
- Like S<sub>N</sub>1, they yield no reaction in apolar solvents.
