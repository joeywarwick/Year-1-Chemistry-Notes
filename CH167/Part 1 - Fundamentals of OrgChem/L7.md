## 2. Intro to mechanisms
- Most reactions occur between a electrophile and a nucleophile.
	- Nucleophiles donate electrons.
		- They are similar to bases.
	- Electrophiles accept electrons.
		- They are similar to acids.
- Electrons are transferred from the nucleophile to the electrophile, which results in the formation of a new bond.
> Mechanisms can be predicted with rules that do not require rote learning.
### 2.1. Bond cleavage
- Bonds can break heterolytically or homolytically:
	- Heterolytic is the uneven sharing of electrons between bonding atoms.
		- This typically forms charged species.
	- Homolytic is the even sharing of electrons between bonding atoms.
		- This typically yields radicals.
#### 2.1.1. Heterolytic bond breaking
- The atoms with the higher electronegativity will typically gain the electron.
	- Lone pair do not necessarily require to be shown unless they are used.

### 2.2. Intermediates
- Reactions typically go through reactive intermediates.
	- $A-B \rightarrow A^+ + B^- \rightarrow products$
	- $A-B \rightarrow A^. + B^. \rightarrow products$
-  The reaction intermediate has higher energy than both A-B and products.
	- The more stable the intermediate, the lower the activation energy barrier.
	- So faster reaction.
#### 2.2.1. Hammond Postulate
- Hammond postulate states that the transition state will be closest in structure to the species it is closest in energy to.
	- So it will be structurally more similar to the intermediate.
### 2.3. Reactivity
- Reactivity describes the movement of electrons
	- A curly arrow forms a bond if it connects two atoms.
	- A curly arrow breaks a bond if it starts from a bond.
- Mechanisms can be predicted based on electronegativity, bond polarisation, electronic configuration, and MOs.
- We can predict the movement of electrons based on a few rules:
	- Electrons start somewhere electron-rich (nucleophiles)
	- Electrons go somewhere electron=poor (electrophiles)
	- It ends up somewhere ideal
	- Charges are balanced on both sides of equation
	- Repeat the rules until the best state is reached.
#### 2.3.1. Electron-richness
- We can choose the best nucleophile in the following order:
	1. Full negative charge
		- Salts like NaOH dissociate go give hydroxide ion.
	2. Lone pair
	3. Multiple-bond
	4. Weak single bonds
#### 2.3.2. Electron-poorness
- We choose the best electrophiles in the following order:
	1. Full positive charge.
		- Positive charge represents an empty orbital
	2. An atom attached to a electronegative atom (C, H, etc.)
		- Their electron density is pulled towards the electronegative atom, giving them a partial positive charge
		- We do not directly attack heteroatoms.
	3. Partial positive charge on C or H.
#### 2.3.3. Idealness of electron destination
- The electrons are at an ideal destination if:
	1. They are quenched by C<sup>+</sup> or H<sup>+</sup>
	2. By breaking a π-bond with a heteroatom so a single bond is left over
	3. By breaking the single bond to a heteroatom so it is disconnected from the molecule.
#### 2.3.4. Charge balancing
- The net charge before the reaction and after the reaction should stay the same.
> **NOTE** A positive and a negative charge yields overall neutral charge.
