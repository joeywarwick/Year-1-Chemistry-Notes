## 2. Arrows
- We use a range of arrows in chemistry.
	- Curly arrows (whole head) show the movement of a **pair** of electrons.
	- Curly arrows (half head), AKA fishhook arrows show the movement of **one** electron.
	- Reaction arrows (straight, full head) show the progress from reactions to products
	- Broken arrows (reaction arrows with two slashes) show reactions that do not occur
	- Equilibrium arrows (two fishhooks that point in opposite directions)
	- Resonance arrows (double-headed reaction arrows) show a pain of structures that are resonance forms of the same molecule
	- Dipole arrow (reaction arrow with a straight line at the other end) show the direction of dipoles.
## 3. Inductive and mesomeric effects
- We need to first consider electronegativity.
### 3.1. Electronegativity
- Electronegativity are a measure of how strongly elements pull electrons towards themselves.
- So when they bond with other atoms with a different electronegativity there will be a dipole.
	- The electrons in the bond will be pulled closer to the more electronegative atom.
### 3.2. Mesomeric effects
- Consider butadiene and methamide.
	- The C-C rotation barrier in butadiene is ~30 kJ mol<sup>-1</sup>
	- The C-N rotation barrier in methamide is ~85 kJ mol<sup>-1</sup>.
> This is significantly higher than C-C for ethane.
- We also observe that the bond lengths are shorter than standard C-C and C-N single bonds.
	- But the C=C and C=O bonds are slightly longer than standard C=C and C=O.
- They are capable of forming conjugated structures over the single bond, giving it a partial single bond character.
	- In butadiene the π-electron in one of the double bond delocalises
	- In methamide the LP on the nitrogen delocalises.
> **NOTE** Here the carbon is sp<sup>2</sup> (see above).
- This is essentially withdrawing and donating π-electrons through the molecules.
	- This leads to polarisation.
- We use mesomeric effect to describe and discuss the electron withdrawing or donating properties of the substituent groups.
	- Where the group is electron-withdrawing (EWG) the effect is **-M**.
	- Where the group is electron-donating (EDG) the effect is **+M**.
- A molecule with a lone pair such as O, N, and S are often EDG, as they can donate their lone pair to be transferred in the molecule. However:
	- If they are part of a multiple bond (C=O, C=N, etc.) they can also act as EWG, because they are good stabilisers of the extra electron charge.
> The mesomeric effect can have a very long range as the effect can transmit down a system of conjugated electrons.
### 3.3. Inductive effects
- Inductive effects arise from a polarised σ bond, which is in turn the result of a electronegativity mismatch.
	- Electrons in a bond will be pulled closer to the more electronegative atom.
		- This effect is notated with partial charges $\delta + \space and \space \delta -$
		- The direction which the electrons are being pulled in can be notated with dipole arrows.
#### 3.3.1. Overall dipole moments
- We can determine the polarity of a molecule using overall dipoles.
	- Consider carbon dioxide, O=C=O.
		- Despite C=O each being a polarised bond, their overall directions cancel, resulting in CO<sub>2</sub> being apolar.
	- Consider water, H-O-H
		- The two O-H bonds in water have their dipoles pointing in the same general direction.
		- So their dipoles add constructively resulting in a net dipole moment
		- So water is polar.
> Solvent polarity can impact reactivity and reaction pathway in organic reactions.
- Similar to mesomeric effect, positive inductive effect is notated as +I, and negative is notated as -I.
### 3.4. Mesomeric vs. inductive effects

| Mesomeric effects | Inductive effects |
| ----------------- | ----------------- |
| π-bonds           | σ-bonds           |
| p-orbital overlap | Bond polarisation |
| Long range        | Short range       |
- The mesomeric effect and inductive effect can work together but this is uncommon as lone-pair atoms (O, N, S) are usually also quite electronegative.
	- Mesomeric effects typically dominates.
## 4. Carbocations
- Carbocations are sp<sup>2</sup> hybridised due to lower energy.
	- So they can overlap with adjacent π-bonds
- This overlap stabilises the carbocation because the charge is being spread out.
- We will also need to consider other factors.
	- An oxygen EDG is not as good as a nitrogen EDG.
		- This is because the nitrogen is less electronegative, so it holds onto the electrons less.
