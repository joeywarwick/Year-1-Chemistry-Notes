## 2. Addition of oxygen nucleophiles
### 2.1. Water
- Water can act as a nucleophile and add to carbonyl compounds.
- This occurs in a similar manner to other nucleophilic additions, which takes place in acidic conditions in aqueous solutions.
#### 2.1.1. Factors affecting equilibrium position
- We know that these reaction go through a carbocation intermediate.
	- So the stability of the carbocation will partially determine yield.
- Consider the following carbonyl compounds:
	- Acetone, MeCOMe
	- Acetaldehyde, MeCHO
	- Formaldehyde, HCHO
	- Trichloroacetaldehyde, CCl<sub>3</sub>CHO
- From acetone to formaldehyde:
	- the degree of hyperconjugation decreases.
	- steric crowding around central carbon decreases.
- Trichloroacetaldehyde is somewhat special. The chlorines are EWGs, so -I increases electrophilicity of carbon.
#### 2.1.2. Mechanism of hydride formation
- The water attacks the electrophilic carbonyl carbon.
	- The water now has a positive charge and the carbonyl oxygen has a negative charge.
- Another water attacks the hydrogen bonded to the positively charged oxygen, quenching the positive charge
	- This protonates the water, forming a hydronium ion
- The hydronium ion is deprotonated by the negatively charged oxygen to give the original water molecule.
> **NOTE** This is a very slow reaction so catalysts are used.
#### 2.1.3. Catalysis of water addition - acid
- The acid protonates the carbonyl oxygen, giving the oxygen a positive charge.
	- This is -I from the carbon, so it becomes more electrophilic.
#### 2.1.4. Catalysis of water addition - base
- Hydroxide is a stronger base than water, so it serves as a better nucleophile for attacking the electrophilic carbon.
### 2.2. Acetal and ketal formation
- This can begin from aldehyde or ketone, and they will react with an alcohol (R-OH) and and an acid (H<sub>3</sub>O<sup>+</sup>).
	- The acid first protonates the carbonyl oxygen, which allows the alcohol to attack the carbon.
	- After the removal of the extra hydrogen on the alcohol, a hemiacetal or hemiketal is formed.
		- Here the OR group oxygen's lone pair delocalises to form a C=O double bond, so the compound has a structure similar to the protonated carbonyl we started with.
	- This process is repeated, forming a full ketal or acetal.
> **NOTE** If the starting reagent is a ketone, the result is a ketal. If the starting reagent is an aldehyde, the result is an acetal.
- This reaction produces large quantities of water, which needs to be continuously removed to drive equilibrium to RHS.
	- We use a **Dean-Stark** apparatus to remove the water.
- We can also form a cyclic acetal using a diol.
## 3. Addition of nitrogen nucleophiles
### 3.1. Formation of imines
- We can replace the carbonyl oxygen with a nitrogen.
	- We can start with either an aldehyde or ketone.
	- We react them with a primary amine and aqueous acid.
- The reaction is somewhat similar to acetal/ketal formation (similarities in italics):
	1. Acid protonates oxygen.*
	2. *Amine attacks, and is then deprotonated by **another amine** to form a neutral intermediate.*
	3. The hydroxy group formed is directly protonated.
	4. The protonated hydroxy group is ejected as water.
	5. C=N *double bond* forms.
	6. Deprotonation occurs again.
### 3.2. Formation of enamines
- Enamines are formed in the same way as imines, but with **secondary amines**.
	1. After (4), the C=N double bond is not stable, as neither of the R groups on the secondary amine is suitable as leaving group.
	2. So a hydrogen on a adjacent carbon to the carbonyl carbon is removes and a C=C bond forms using the electron in unstable the C=N double bond.
## 4. Reducing carbonyl groups
- Carbonyls are reduced back to the original carbon using sodium borohydride (NaBH<sub>3</sub>.)
	- This reaction is done in ethanol, EtOH.
	- This is because the alcohol can form H-bonds between the hydroxy group and the carbonyl oxygen.
		- This activates the oxygen.
- The reaction progresses as follows:
	- The BH<sub>3</sub><sup>-</sup> dissociates into a hydride and a borane.
	- The hydride attacks the electrophilic carbon, pushing a negative charge onto the oxygen.
	- The borane now reacts with the anion.
		- This repeats four times to form B(OR)<sub>4</sub><sup>-</sup>.
			- So four carbonyls are bonded to the boron.
	- Then the boron anion reacts with acid to give boric acid and the alcohol.
