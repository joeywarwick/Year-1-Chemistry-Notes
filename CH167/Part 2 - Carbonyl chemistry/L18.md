## 2. Hydrolysis of esters and amides (cont.)
- Different esters require different approaches.
- Consider the ester RCOOC(Me)<sub>2</sub>Me.
	- The tert-butyl group is large and sterically hinders access to the carbonyl carbon.
- So after the protonation of the carbonyl oxygen water **cannot** attack directly.
	- Instead, the C-O bond on the tert-butyl side breaks, forming the carboxylic acid directly.
	- The tert-butyl becomes a carbocation.
		- This is quite favourable because it is a tertiary carbocation.
	- The water attacks the carbocation instead to give tert-butyl alcohol.
> This is A<sub>AL</sub>1 mechanism
### 2.1. Nomenclature of mechanisms
- Similar to S<sub>N</sub> reactions, these hydrolysis reactions also have their own nomenclature.
	- The 'Reverse-Fischer' hydrolysis was termed A<sub>AC</sub>2 (see L17) and this was termed A<sub>AL</sub>1. 
	- A<sub>AC</sub>2:
		- A: Acid-cataslysed
		- AC: Acyl-oxygen C-O bond broken
		- 2: RDS is bimolecular.
	- A<sub>AL</sub>1:
		- A: Acid-catalysed
		- AL: Alcohol-oxygen C-O bond broken
		- 1: RDS is unimolecular.
### 2.2. Based-catalysed hydrolysis of esters
- B<sub>AC</sub>2
	- B: Base
- Base can also catalyse hydrolysis of esters in a simpler reaction.
- This is essentially a substitution reaction where the hydroxide group replaces the OMe methoxide group.
- This is **irreversible**. 
	- This is because the carboxylate base formed is very stable from conjugation.
### 2.3. Hydrolysing amides
- Amides are hydrolysed under acid catalysis.
- The reaction proceed as follows:
	1. Carbonyl oxygen protonated
	2. Water attacks
	3. Water deprotonated.
	4. Nitrogen protonated
	5. C=OH bond forms, ejects amine
	6. Deprotonation of C=OH
	7. The amine is protonated by acid to give ammonium cation.
	8. It reacts with the carboxylic acid to give ammonium carboxylate salt
### 2.4. Reduction of esters with aluminium hydride
- We previously used sodium borohydride to reduce from more reactive carbonyl centres like aldehyde and ketones.
- With less reactive esters we use lithium aluminium hydride (LiAlH<sub>4</sub>).
	- This is because NaBH<sub>3</sub> is intrinsically insufficiently reactive.
- The reaction proceeds as followed:
	1. A hydrogen on aluminium hydride anion attacks the carbonyl centre.
	2. C=O reforms, OR group ejected to bond with the AlH<sub>3</sub>.
- Here an aldehyde forms, but we can continue to reduce to alcohol.
	- To prevent this we use at least6 2:1 ester to hydride so all is mopped up.
	1. The AlH<sub>3</sub>OR<sup>-</sup> now attack the carbonyl centre again.
	2. The negative charge on the carbonyl oxygen attacks the LiH<sub>2</sub>OR
	3. This links the two molecules, with a negative charge on the Al.
	4. EtOAc and acid is added to liberate the free alcohol.
## 3. Oxidation levels
- We use oxidation levels, which are arbitrary numbers to describe the extent of oxidation.
- This number is essentially the number of bonds between carbon and heteroatoms.
- Consider the following compounds:
	- Methane
	- Methanol
	- Formaldehyde
	- Formic acid
	- Dimethyl carbonate.
- They are in increasing levels of oxidation.
	- Methane 0
	- Methanol 1
	- Formaldehyde 2
	- Formic acid 3
	- Dimethyl carbonate 4.
