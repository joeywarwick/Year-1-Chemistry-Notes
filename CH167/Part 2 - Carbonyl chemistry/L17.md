## 2. Synthesis of carboxylic acid derivatives (cont.)
- We might also need acid anhydrides from time to time.
### 2.1. Synthesis of acid anhydrides
- Synthesising an acid anhydride requires a carboxylic acid and an acid chloride.
	- This is a **base-catalysed** reaction.
- The reaction proceeds as follows:
	1. The hydroxide ion attacks the hydroxyl hydrogen, giving the oxygen a negative charge.
	2. The oxygen attacks the carbon centre of the acid chloride, giving its oxygen a negative charge.
	3. Chlorine is chosen as the leaving group.
	4. π-bond reforms between O and C.
## 3. Esters and amides
- Ester and amides can both be made from acid chlorides.
### 3.1. Esters
- The ester is made from the acid chloride.
	- An acyl chloride is reacted with an alcohol
	- This is done in the presence of triethylamine (Et<sub>3</sub>N).
- The reaction proceeds as followed:
	1. Alcohol oxygen attacks carbonyl carbon.
		- This pushes a negative charge onto the oxygen.
	2. Ethylamine deprotonates the oxygen.
	3. Chloride ejected as leaving group
	4. π-bond reforms between oxygen and carbon.
> **NOTE** The chlorine cannot be ejected first, this is because the oxygen has no LP and is very unstable, so must be deprotonated first.
#### 3.1.1. Esters from carboxylic acids
- Esters can also be formed from carboxylic acids even though carboxylic acids are less reactive.
	- This is the **Fischer Esterification**.
- The reaction conditions are more harsh than acyl chlorides:
	- Carboxylic acid reacts with an alcohol
		- The alcohol is in **large excess** to drive the equilibrium. 
		- As a result, it is usually the solvent.
	- Under **acid catalysis**.
> The reaction is like a 'crossover' between acetal formation and esterification with acyl chlorides.
- It proceeds as follows:
	1. The carbonyl oxygen is protonated.
	2. Alcohol oxygen attacks carbonyl carbon
	3. Alcohol deprotonates the positive oxygen
	4. One of the hydroxy groups are protonated
	5. C=O<sup>+</sup>H of the unprotonated hydroxyl forms, protonated OH ejected as water
	6. C=O<sup>+</sup>H deprotonated
### 3.2. Amides
- Amides are formed in a similar mechanism.
	- An acyl chloride is reacted with an amine
> **NOTE** Two equivalents of the amine is used to take the role of triethylamine in ester formation.
- The reaction is identical on other aspects.
## 4. Hydrolysis of carboxylic acid derivatives
### 4.1. Hydrolysis of acid chlorides and anhydrides
- Acid chloride and acid anhydrides are hydrolysed in the same manner.
- They will react directly with water.
	1. Water attacks the carbon
	2. Deprotonation with another water
	3. Reformation of C=O, ejection of leaving group
> **NOTE** Again, the positively charged O is very unstable so deprotonation must occur first.
### 4.2. Hydrolysis of esters and amides
- They can be hydrolysed simply in the presence of water.
- But this is very slow.
	- The resonance forms stabilise the ester/amide greatly.
		- The mesomeric effect reduces the partial positive charge on 
	- A catalytic acid is used.
- The reaction proceeds as follwing:
	1. Protonation of the carbonyl oxygen
	2. Attack by water
	3. Deprotonation of water oxygen
	4. Ester oxygen protonated
	5. Ester C-O bond breaks heterolytically, yielding the alcohol.
	6. Water deprotonates the C=O<sup>+</sup>H.
> **NOTE** This is the reverse of Fischer esterification. This is known as the A<sub>AC</sub>2 mechanism.
