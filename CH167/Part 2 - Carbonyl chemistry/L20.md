## 2. Formation and stability of enolates
- While ketones form enols, aldehydes can form enolates.
- In base:
	1. Hydroxide attacks an α-proton
	2. Charge pushed onto carbonyl carbon
	- This has a full negative charge hence enolate.
- Again, the as water is a weaker base the equilibrium will be towards the LHS.
	- So small K
- The quantitative size of K is dependent on the base's strength. 
	- If a sufficiently strong base is used we can yield almost entirely enolate.
- A strong base used to completely deprotonate is LDA.
	- The nitrogen centre attacks the α-proton to yield enolate.
- The lithium dissociates and reacts with the enolate.
### 2.1. Enolates from esters
- We do not need a base as strong as LDA, so NaOMe, sodium methoxide, is used.
	- Again, the methoxide anion attacks an α-proton.
- Here the methoxide can also be added nucleophilically to the carbonyl.
	- The leaving group can vary between the methoxide and the ester alcohol, so it may be complicated and forms side products.
## 3. Reactivity of ketones, enols, and enolates
- Ketones react as previously stated with nucleophiles to form an adduct.
- Enols react in the same manner as alkenes, and can react with electrophiles.
	- Note that the positive charge after reaction can either be in the form of a carbocation or a C=O<sup>+</sup>H bond.
- Enolates are a more reactive form of enols with the full negative charge.
	- The electrophile can add directly to the oxygen or the α-carbon depending on the resonance structure.
### 3.1. Enol bromination
- We can brominate enols, like alkenes, under acidic conditions.
	- In this reaction the enol is the **nucleophile**.
- The bromine is attacked by the C=C bond electrons, adding a bromine.
	- The hydroxy group can form C=OH to stabilise the carbocation with the oxygen LP on the other side.
- Here the oxygen is deprotonated by the bromine to give bromoketone and HBr. 
> **NOTE** We do not form a dibromoalcohol!
- The yield of the bromoketone, however is low, as the enol only exists in small quantities (see L19).
#### 3.1.1. Making stable enols
- Recall that we can use strong bases to make good yields of enols and enolates.
	- We do not use LDA, but instead trimethylsilane chloride (Me<sub>3</sub>SiCl)
- After the Li-enolate salt forms, the oxygen attacks the silicon.
	- This displaces the chloride and removes the lithium cation.
	- So gives Me<sub>3</sub>Si-enolate.
	- $Me_3SiCl +  RCHCOLi(R) \rightarrow Me_3SiOC(R)CHR + LiCl$
### 3.2. Enolates and electrophiles
- Enolates also react with electrophiles.
	- As stated above, they can add to either the oxygen or the C=C bond.
- Consider the reaction of propen-2-olate with an alkyl bromide.
	- The major products are the result of of carbon attack on the alkyl chain.
#### 3.2.1. Hard and soft nucleophiles/electrophiles
- We can evaluate the likelihood of attack location by considering the hardness of the sites
	- We say that the oxygen is a hard site
	- We say that the carbon is a soft site.
- Hard sites are where there is:
	- A full negative charge localised on one atom.
		- e.g. Methoxides with all negative charge on oxygen
- Soft sites are where there is:
	- A partial negative charge; or
		- e.g. Amine nitrogens
	- Dispersing of the electrons used for attack between atoms.
		- This is the case here, where the electrons used are in the C=C bond between two carbon atoms.
- Hard electrophiles react at hard nucleophiles, soft electrophiles react at soft nucleophiles.
- Similarly, hard electrophiles are where there is:
	- A full localised positive charge.
		- e.g. protons
- Soft electrophiles are where there is:
	- A partial positive charge.
		- e.g. The alkyl group in this case.
### 3.3. 1,3-dicarbonyl compounds
- The location of deprotonation determines the acidity of the enol.
	- Consider pentan-2,4,-dione, MeCOCHCOMe.
		- If the deprotonation occurs at the ends, pK<sub>a</sub> = 20.
		- If the deprotonation occurs at the middle, pK<sub>a</sub> = 10.
	- This is because there is greater resonance available at centre.
- So in these cases only weak bases are required to fully deprotonate.
	- We can use NaOMe
- We cannot quench this with water, because the pK<sub>a</sub> is lower than water.
	- We must quench with HCl or similar.
#### 3.3.1. Alkylation of 1,3-dicarbonyl compounds
- Alkylation is done with NaOMe to enolise, then reacting with R-I or R-Br to yield the alkyl.
	- Remember that the preferred location is between the carbonyls.
- If it is an ester the reaction is the same, and the ester OMe is basically disregarded.
#### 3.3.2. Decarboxylation of 1,3,-dicarbonyl compounds
- Decarboxylation **is done with an ester**.
	1. The ester is first hydrolysed back to the carboxylic acid.
		- This is the 1,3-ketoacid
			- There will be intramolecular hydrogen bonding between the acid and the ketone.
	2. Enol is then formed upon heating, where the C=O breaks to attack the O-H hydrogen, forming the C=O bond, and breaking the C-C bond.
		- Here enol/ketone and carbon dioxide is evolved.
